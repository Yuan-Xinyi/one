\begin{abstract}
This work studies \textit{extreme motion generation}, which aims to maximize the Cartesian path length that a manipulator executes along a prescribed path. Conventional reachability analysis indicates whether each point of a path is attainable. However, it does not indicate how far a \emph{single continuous joint trajectory} can carry the tool before a joint limit, an orientation tolerance, or a collision terminates the motion, and the two quantities differ substantially. We quantify the realizable part as \textit{continuous kinematic reachability} and bracket it per task between a numerical pointwise-reachability estimate and the executed stroke. \new{To realize a large fraction of the bracket, we formulate stroke maximization as a discounted exit-time optimal control problem and show that the pointwise Hamiltonian maximization is attained on the vertices of the null-space command box, so every deployed controller switches among vertex commands according to a scalar field of the configuration. A training-free constraint-margin field already matches receding-horizon optimization of its own objective, and the critic of a standard reinforcement-learning run further improves the executed stroke at identical per-step computation, whereas the policy head of the same run remains far below its own critic consumed in this way. The initial configuration is chosen by a learned selector supervised by rollouts of the deployed controller.} We evaluate the proposed framework on three manipulators with 10,000 path-following simulation tasks each, together with real-world validations, and further show that the same measurement provides a task-aware reference for manipulator base placement.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}




\IEEEPARstart{C}{onsider} a compact, fixed-base manipulator executing a coating or welding operation over a large work surface. Intuition would suggest that the farthest reachable point is determined by the reachable workspace. In practice, however, the motion is continuous, and one of the safety constraints, such as joint limits, tool-orientation tolerance, or collision, is violated long before the end-effector approaches the workspace boundary. Consequently, the achievable path length is decided by how long those boundaries can be deferred during execution. We refer to the problem of maximizing the Cartesian path length along a prescribed path as \textit{extreme motion generation}. This is particularly important in industrial automation, where the length of a single continuous motion directly limits the area a manipulator can process in one setup. Rather than scaling up the manipulator or repeatedly relocating the base, exploiting the extreme motion enlarges the effective working range\footnote{The project website and related videos of this paper can be found at \url{https://yuan-xinyi.github.io/extreme-motion-generation/}.}.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{imgs/motivation_fig.png}
    \caption{Pointwise reachability overestimates continuous reachability. From each point on the plane $z=0.2\,\mathrm{m}$, the tool moves along $+x$ under a $30^{\circ}$ cone and self-collision constraints. (a) Farthest distance with pointwise-valid configurations; (b) farthest distance achievable by a single continuous joint trajectory. The median achievable length drops from $0.71$ to $0.46\,\mathrm{m}$.}

\new{The outcome of an extended stroke is determined by two coupled choices that no reachability map captures. The first is made once, before the motion begins: which branch of the task-feasible configuration set the stroke starts on, a discrete choice no continuous motion can revise. The second is made at every control step: how the redundancy is resolved, since the joint motion chosen now steers which constraint the arm meets tens of increments later. Moreover, the horizon itself, namely when the stroke ends, is exactly the quantity being optimized. Therefore, we formulate the problem as a discounted \emph{exit-time} optimal control problem.}

\new{The exit-time formulation provides three benefits. First, its Hamilton--Jacobi--Bellman (HJB) equation is affine in the box-bounded null-space command, so the pointwise maximization of its Hamiltonian is attained on the $2^{m}$ vertices of the command box, and the greedy policy with respect to any differentiable value estimate can be taken vertex-valued. Every controller in this paper therefore shares one form, an exact one-step model evaluating a scalar potential, and differs only in how much future knowledge the potential carries. Second, the formulation identifies why the classical law underperforms: it reads a hand-weighted instantaneous surrogate. Substituting the analytic \emph{constraint-margin field} for the intractable $V$ yields a training-free law that already matches receding-horizon optimization of its own objective. Third, and most importantly, the formulation prescribes how learned long-horizon knowledge should be consumed. A reinforcement-learning run trains a policy head and a critic on the same experience. Deployed directly, the policy must compress the value gradient into its own weights and stays below the training-free law. In contrast, the \emph{critic} of the identical run, consumed as the scalar field of the same one-step law, outperforms every other controller at identical per-step computation: the exact model separates the candidate actions one step apart, and the smooth learned value ranks the resulting successors, whose correlated errors largely cancel. In short, learning supplies smooth state values, and the exact model supplies the fine local distinctions.}

\new{We evaluate the proposed framework on path-following tasks across three manipulators, measuring performance against the pointwise-reachable length of each task so that the gap between pointwise and continuous reachability is itself quantified. Ablation studies verify the mechanism: the optimization horizon pays off only on the learned field, the gain requires a value trained toward optimal continuations, and further policy-iteration rounds on top of the deployed critic add nothing. The selector also transfers to serpentine and non-planar path families it has never seen.}

\new{An earlier version of this work, restricted to one manipulator with a generative initial-configuration model and a best-of-$K$ reference, is available as a preprint~\cite{yuan2026extreme}; this article differs in the measurement, the trajectory stage (derived rather than trained), the candidate layer, and scope.}

\new{In summary, the main contributions of this work are as follows.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \new{We formulate stroke maximization as a discounted exit-time optimal control problem whose HJB Hamiltonian is affine in the box-bounded null-space command. The greedy policy with respect to any differentiable value estimate can therefore be taken on the $2^{m}$ vertices, every deployable controller becomes an exact one-step model evaluating a scalar potential, and the classical law is absorbed into the same family. The analytic constraint-margin field yields a training-free law that matches receding-horizon optimization of its own objective and transfers across manipulators without retuning.}
\item \new{We identify and measure a division of labor between learning and the exact model: from one reinforcement-learning run, the policy head deployed directly stays below the training-free margin law, whereas the critic consumed by the one-step vertex law outperforms every compared controller at identical per-step computation on all three manipulators, with no additional training. Ablation studies attribute the gain to the value itself.}
\item \new{We develop a two-sided numerical bracket of continuous kinematic reachability. A best-of-$K$ reference is not sound, as achieved strokes are observed to exceed it. Instead, we march along each task's own path and search for an admissible configuration at each sample, with the search made witness-consistent so that the upper side is never below any evaluated rollout, and with its sensitivity to the search budget quantified. The gap between pointwise and continuous reachability is then measured per task, across three manipulators, and over a distribution of tasks. The same inexpensive query also corrects a systematic optimism in reachability-map-based base placement.}
\item \new{We design a learned selector for the initial branch, the one decision a resolution law cannot make. Every candidate is rolled out under the deployed law, the outcomes serve as the labels, and a permutation-equivariant selector conditioned on a sampled table of the path ahead chooses in one forward pass. The supervision costs nothing beyond the rollouts, and the selector transfers to path families it has never seen.}
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Local Dexterity, Reachability, and Global Resolution}
Manipulability characterizes local dexterity through the
Jacobian~\cite{yoshikawa1985manipulability,yoshikawa1985dynamic,chiu1988task,klein1987dexterity}
and has been folded into null-space
resolution~\cite{liegeois1977automatic,jin2017manipulability,jaquier2021geometry,haviland2021neo}
and planning~\cite{shen2023adaptive,cai2025just}; workspace reachability
characterizes global feasibility through capability
maps~\cite{yang2009placement,zacharias2007capturing} used for base
placement~\cite{makhal2018reuleaux,vahrenkamp2013robot} and learned
priors~\cite{feng2025predictive,jauhri2022robot,selim2022safe}. Neither
indicates how joint motion should be coordinated so that a single
continuous path approaches the reachable boundary. Closer to that
question, the global structure of the solution set is well understood:
redundant IK solutions form disjoint self-motion
manifolds~\cite{burdick1989inverse}, joint limits split them into
disconnected branches~\cite{shimizu2008analytical,luck1993self}, and
global resolution has been posed as continuous
pseudoinversion~\cite{hauser2018global} or dynamic programming across
homotopy classes~\cite{ferrentino2020optimal,ferrentino2021globally,fabregat2025topological};
extremal solutions are globally optimal within their own
class~\cite{martin1989resolution,suh1987local,kazerounian1988global}.
These works treat the Cartesian path as given and ask how to execute it
optimally. They do not ask how far the path can be extended, nor how much
of the achievable extent is decided by the branch as against the law
applied within it, measured over a distribution of tasks. Learning-based
resolution~\cite{malik2022deep} and generative IK
solvers~\cite{ames2022ikflow,zhang2025ikdiffuser,huang2024diffusionseeder,yoon2023learning}
model the multiplicity of solutions but are likewise evaluated on how
well a prescribed path is executed. In contrast, this paper assigns
learning to state-level judgments (branch selection and successor
evaluation) and the exact model to action-level distinctions; learned
reactive policies are evaluated as baselines under identical conditions.

\subsection{\new{Quantifying Continuous Reachability}}
\new{Two lines of work are closely related to this measurement, and we
state the differences explicitly. The first line asks whether a
prescribed path can be executed at all by one continuous joint
trajectory, including the hierarchy of pointwise, pathwise and global
resolution~\cite{hauser2016continuous}, paths whose waypoints are
individually reachable while no continuous resolution
exists~\cite{hauser2018global}, and the $t$-connected regions of the
cuspidal literature~\cite{wenger1991uniqueness,wenger2023cuspidal,elias2025cuspidal}.
These are set-valued or binary statements. The second line counts interruptions when a
path cannot be covered entirely, minimizing reconfigurations or
breakpoints~\cite{wang2024iklink,yang2022optimal,yin2024breakpoints}.
However, a count yields no arc length and no upper reference, and by
permitting reconfiguration it cannot ask how far a \emph{single} stroke
reaches. Closest in spirit, Gautier et al.~\cite{gautier2024comparison}
reported maximum weldable extents along prescribed trajectories, but as
case-study numbers for a non-redundant arm, which are one-sided by
construction. Therefore, a per-task, two-sided, distribution-level
measurement, together with the attribution of the measured extent to the
choices that produce it, is still missing; supplying it is one goal of
this paper.}

\section{Problem Formulation}
While extreme motion generation applies broadly to constrained path following, we here specify it as extreme straight-line path following. Specifically, a fixed-base manipulator advances its end-effector along the line with a tool-orientation constraint. Solving it requires choosing the initial joint configuration and then generating the motion that maximizes the achievable path length.



Formally, consider a manipulator with $n$ degrees of freedom, where $\mathbf{q} \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$ denotes the joint configuration. Let $\mathbf{p}(\mathbf{q}) \in \mathbb{R}^{3}$ denote the end-effector position and $\mathbf{z}(\mathbf{q}) \in \mathbb{R}^{3}$ denote the TCP local $z$-axis. A straight-line path-following task is specified by the condition vector $\mathbf{c} = [\mathbf{p}_{0}, \mathbf{d}, \mathbf{n}] \in \mathbb{R}^{9}$, where $\mathbf{p}_{0}$ is the starting position and $\mathbf{d}$, $\mathbf{n}$ are unit vectors giving the motion direction and task-plane normal. \new{The prescribed path is the ray $\mathcal{P}=\{\mathbf{p}_{0}+s\,\mathbf{d} : s\ge 0\}$, along which the end-effector advances at a commanded speed $v$, one command being issued per control period $\Delta t$.} \new{The straight ray is the default member of a family sharing this condition vector: a signed curvature bends it into an arc, a sinusoidal lateral offset turns it into a serpentine seam, and a rotating cone axis $\mathbf{n}(s)$ models a non-planar workpiece. All four families are evaluated in Section~\ref{sec:setup}; the formulation carries over with $\mathbf{d}$ read as the instantaneous tangent and $\mathbf{n}$ as the local cone axis.}

Starting from an initial configuration $\mathbf{q}_{0}^{*}$, the policy $\pi$ chooses the joint motion at every control step, producing a joint trajectory that ends at step $T$, when a safety constraint is first violated. \new{The executed motion is a \emph{stroke} of Cartesian length $\mathcal{L}$.} The task is the constrained optimization problem:
\begin{align}
\max_{\mathbf{q}_{0}^{*},\,\pi} \quad & \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{q}^{*}_{0},\,\pi;\,\mathbf{p}_{0}, \mathbf{d}, \mathbf{n}) \\
\text{s.t.} \quad & {\color{red}\operatorname{dist}\bigl(\mathbf{p}(\mathbf{q}_{t}^{*}),\,\mathcal{P}\bigr) \leq \epsilon_{\text{pos}}}, \label{eq:pos_constraint} \\
& \arccos\!\left( \mathbf{z}(\mathbf{q}_{t}^{*})^{\top} \mathbf{n} \right) \leq \theta_{\max}, \label{eq:ori_constraint} \\
& \mathbf{q}_{\min} \preceq \mathbf{q}_{t}^{*} \preceq \mathbf{q}_{\max}, \label{eq:jl_constraint}
\end{align}
where $\mathcal{L}$ denotes the stroke length, $\mathbf{q}_0^*$ and $\pi$ are the initial configuration and the control policy to be optimized, and $(\mathbf{p}_0, \mathbf{d}, \mathbf{n})$ specify the task conditions as above. The $\epsilon_{\text{pos}}$, $\theta_{\max}$, and $[\mathbf{q}_{\min}, \mathbf{q}_{\max}]$ are the position tolerance, orientation tolerance, and joint limits, respectively.

Since only the $3$-DoF position must be tracked, the manipulator is kinematically redundant for $n>3$, and the commanded joint velocity decomposes into a task term and a null-space term,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:nullspace}
{\color{red}
\dot{\mathbf{q}} = \mathbf{J}^{+}_{\lambda}\dot{\mathbf{x}} + \mathbf{B}(\mathbf{q})\,\mathbf{u},
\qquad
\mathbf{J}\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{0},\quad
\mathbf{B}^{\!\top}\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{I},}
\end{equation}
\new{where $\mathbf{J}^{+}_{\lambda}$ is the damped pseudo-inverse of the
position Jacobian and $\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{q})$ an orthonormal basis of its
\emph{exact} null space (double-precision SVD; task-aligned construction in
Section~\ref{sec:traj}), so $\mathbf{J}\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{0}$ holds by
construction and the secondary motion cannot disturb the end-effector
regardless of the damping; $\mathbf{u}$ collects the null-space coordinates.}
Within this null space the classical law performs gradient ascent on a
weighted secondary field,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:secondary}
\boldsymbol{\xi}=k_{\mu}\,\nabla_{\mathbf{q}}w_{\mathbf{d}}(\mathbf{q})\;-\;k_{\mathrm{jl}}\,\frac{\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{q}_{\mathrm{mid}}}{\mathbf{q}_{\max}-\mathbf{q}_{\min}}\;+\;k_{\theta}\,g(\theta)\,\nabla_{\mathbf{q}}\!\big(\mathbf{z}(\mathbf{q})^{\top}\mathbf{n}\big),
\end{equation}
where the secondary field $\boldsymbol{\xi}$ combines a directional-manipulability term $w_{\mathbf{d}}$ that retreats from singularities along $\mathbf{d}$, a centering term that pulls each joint toward the middle $\mathbf{q}_{\mathrm{mid}}$ of its range, and a cone term gated by $g(\theta)$ that activates only as the tool axis approaches the $\theta_{\max}$ boundary. \new{It enters the decomposition as $\mathbf{u}\propto\mathbf{B}^{\!\top}\boldsymbol{\xi}$; all controllers share Eq.~(\ref{eq:nullspace}) and differ only in how $\mathbf{u}$ is generated.}

{\color{red}
Note that the horizon of this problem is not given in advance: the stroke ends at the first constraint violation, and the time at which this happens is itself determined by the commands being optimized. The next section casts the objective as a discounted \emph{exit-time} optimal control problem and derives the proposed resolution laws from it.
}

\section{\texorpdfstring{\new{Extreme Motion Generation}}{Extreme Motion Generation}}
\label{sec:hjb}

\new{This section presents the proposed framework. We first cast stroke maximization as a discounted exit-time optimal control problem, from which the vertex-valued resolution laws are derived: the pointwise Hamiltonian maximization is attained on the $2^{m}$ vertices of the command box, the classical law belongs to the same family as the optimal one, and substituting deployable scalar fields yields the analytic margin law and the learned value law. We then present the two deployed stages, trajectory generation and initial configuration selection. Both stages instantiate one division of labor: learning is asked only for smooth, state-level judgments, while the fine distinctions between actions are computed by the exact model at decision time.}

{\color{red}
\subsection{Exit-Time Optimal Control Formulation}
Collect the constraints of
Eqs.~(\ref{eq:pos_constraint})--(\ref{eq:jl_constraint}), together with the
self-collision clearance, into an open feasible set $\Omega$ of configurations,
and let $\tau = \inf\{t \ge 0 : \mathbf{q}(t) \notin \Omega\}$ denote the exit time of a closed-loop trajectory (not to be confused with the switching thresholds of Section~\ref{sec:traj}). Throughout this work the null-space coordinates
are bounded, $\mathbf{u} = a_{\max}\,\mathbf{a}$ with
$\mathbf{a} \in \mathcal{A} := [-1,1]^{m}$, where $m = n-3$ is the dimension
of the null space left by the position task ($m=4$ for the two 7-DoF arms,
$m=3$ for Cobotta). Under this bound the decomposition of
Eq.~(\ref{eq:nullspace}) is control-affine,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:affine}
\dot{\mathbf{q}} \;=\; \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{q}) \;+\; \mathbf{G}(\mathbf{q})\,\mathbf{a},
\qquad
\mathbf{f} := \mathbf{J}^{+}_{\lambda}\dot{\mathbf{x}},\quad
\mathbf{G} := a_{\max}\,\mathbf{B},
\end{equation}
with the task condition $\mathbf{c}$ entering $\mathbf{f}$ and $\Omega$ as a
fixed parameter. Writing $r(\mathbf{q}) \ge 0$ for the instantaneous advance
rate along $\mathbf{d}$, i.e., the continuous-time counterpart of the reward
defined in Section~\ref{sec:traj}, define the discounted progress value
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:value}
V(\mathbf{q}_{0}) \;=\; \sup_{\mathbf{a}(\cdot)}
\int_{0}^{\tau} e^{-\beta t}\, r(\mathbf{q}(t))\,\mathrm{d}t,
\end{equation}
where the supremum runs over measurable commands into $\mathcal{A}$ and $\beta>0$ matches the implementation's discount. On the straight-line family the task term holds the advance rate at $v$, so $V$ increases with the exit time alone: the discounted problem is a maximum-exit-time problem. $V$ in (\ref{eq:value}) is the optimal value; the learned components of this paper approximate values of \emph{policies}. The analysis below uses only two facts: the dynamics (\ref{eq:affine}) are affine in $\mathbf{a}$, and the advance rate $r$ does not depend on $\mathbf{a}$ because $\mathbf{J}\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{0}$ holds exactly. What separates the exit-time form from instantaneous null-space optimization is only the argument of the maximization: a command at $\mathbf{q}$ is judged by how it changes all future feasible progress through $V$, rather than by any quantity evaluable at $\mathbf{q}$ in isolation.

\subsection{Vertex-Valued Feedback}
At points of differentiability the value function (\ref{eq:value}) satisfies the HJB relation of the discounted exit-time problem,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:hjb}
\beta V \;=\; r \;+\; \nabla V^{\!\top}\mathbf{f}
\;+\; \sup_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{A}}
\bigl(\mathbf{G}^{\!\top}\nabla V\bigr)^{\!\top}\mathbf{a},
\qquad V \equiv 0 \ \text{outside } \Omega .
\end{equation}
Because $r$ and $\mathbf{f}$ do not depend on $\mathbf{a}$, the command enters
only through the last term, linearly. Define the \emph{switching vector}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sigma}
\boldsymbol{\sigma}(\mathbf{q}) \;:=\;
\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{q})^{\!\top}\nabla V(\mathbf{q}) \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{m},
\end{equation}
whose $i$-th component is the marginal effect of the $i$-th null-space direction on discounted future feasible progress. The supremum in (\ref{eq:hjb}) equals $\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}\|_{1}$ and, wherever no component vanishes, is attained at the single vertex $\mathbf{a} = \operatorname{sgn}\boldsymbol{\sigma}$: the resolution implied by the value function selects an orthant, not a magnitude.

The observation above does not require $V$ to be the optimal value function;
it holds for the gradient field of any scalar function of the configuration,
which makes it usable even when the value estimate is inexact.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:vertex}
Let $W:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}$ be differentiable and
$\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{W} := \mathbf{G}^{\!\top}\nabla W$. Then, at every
$\mathbf{q}$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:prop}
\max_{\mathbf{a}\in[-1,1]^{m}} \boldsymbol{\sigma}_{W}^{\!\top}\mathbf{a}
\;=\;
\max_{\mathbf{a}\in\{-1,1\}^{m}} \boldsymbol{\sigma}_{W}^{\!\top}\mathbf{a}
\;=\; \|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{W}\|_{1},
\end{equation}
attained at $\mathbf{a}=\operatorname{sgn}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{W}$, with the
components on which $\sigma_{W,i}=0$ chosen arbitrarily in $\{-1,1\}$.
Consequently the greedy policy with respect to any differentiable value
estimate can be taken vertex-valued: the vertex action space is closed under
policy improvement.
\end{proposition}

\begin{IEEEproof}
$\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{W}^{\!\top}\mathbf{a}
= \sum_{i}\sigma_{W,i}\,a_{i} \le \sum_{i}|\sigma_{W,i}|$ for
$\mathbf{a}\in[-1,1]^{m}$, with equality iff
$a_{i}=\operatorname{sgn}\sigma_{W,i}$ on every component with
$\sigma_{W,i}\neq 0$; such an $\mathbf{a}$ can be chosen in $\{-1,1\}^{m}$.
\end{IEEEproof}

\begin{remark}[Scope]
\label{rem:bangbang}
Three delimitations. The box- and vertex-constrained problems share their pointwise Hamiltonian by (\ref{eq:prop}); equality of the value functions would need viscosity comparison conditions we neither verify nor use. Where components of $\boldsymbol{\sigma}$ vanish, an optimal vertex-valued \emph{selector exists} rather than every optimal command being a vertex. And the reduction is exact in continuous time only; the finite-rate gap is answered empirically in Section~\ref{sec:setup}, and the box itself is a design choice with no canonical status.
\end{remark}



This structural conclusion is the kinematic counterpart of a classical result: in time-optimal control along a prescribed path, the torque box with control-affine dynamics forces the optimal acceleration to the boundary of the admissible set except on singular arcs~\cite{bobrow1985time,shin1985minimum}.

\subsection{From the Classical Law to the Analytic Margin Law}
\label{sec:relation}
The classical secondary command of Eq.~(\ref{eq:secondary}) performs gradient
ascent on a weighted instantaneous objective; writing that objective, gains
and $a_{\max}$ absorbed, as $\Phi(\mathbf{q})$, the two laws read, in the
shared basis and up to scaling,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cl-vs-opt}
\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{cl}}(\mathbf{q})
= \operatorname{clip}\bigl(\mathbf{G}^{\!\top}\nabla\Phi(\mathbf{q})\bigr),
\qquad
\mathbf{a}^{*}(\mathbf{q})
= \operatorname{sgn}\bigl(\mathbf{G}^{\!\top}\nabla V(\mathbf{q})\bigr).
\end{equation}
They differ in exactly two substitutions: the scalar field ($\Phi$ a hand-designed surrogate at the current configuration, $V$ the discounted future feasible progress) and the use of the gradient (the classical law keeps its magnitude, so the command shrinks where the surrogate is flat; the value-based feedback keeps only its sign).

$V$ itself has no closed form, and its domain, configurations crossed with task conditions, is far beyond the reach of grid-based HJB solvers. However, the exit-time structure indicates what the value gradient should attend to: on this family $V$ increases with the exit time alone, and the exit is caused by the first margin that reaches zero. This motivates replacing $V$ by the \emph{constraint-margin field}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:margin-field}
\Phi_{\mathrm{m}}(\mathbf{q})
= -\tau_{\mathrm{m}} \log \sum_{j} \exp\bigl(-m_{j}(\mathbf{q})/\tau_{\mathrm{m}}\bigr),
\end{equation}
a soft minimum over the normalized joint-limit and orientation-cone margins
$m_{j}$, i.e., the constraints whose exhaustion terminates strokes. The
deployed training-free law is the vertex law of
Proposition~\ref{prop:vertex} applied to this field,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:margin-law}
\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{m}}(\mathbf{q})
= \operatorname{sgn}\bigl(\mathbf{G}^{\!\top}\nabla\Phi_{\mathrm{m}}(\mathbf{q})\bigr),
\end{equation}
optionally in its finite-rate form, where the $2^{m}$ vertices are scored
by $\Phi_{\mathrm{m}}$ one held command ahead (see
Remark~\ref{rem:bangbang} for the finite-rate gap). Relative to the
classical law, this law reverses
both substitutions of (\ref{eq:cl-vs-opt}): the field measures proximity to
\emph{all} exits jointly, and the sign keeps full authority where the field
is flat. It has no trained parameters and no per-manipulator gains.

\subsection{From the Margin Field to a Learned Value Field}
\label{sec:vlook}
The margin field only evaluates the \emph{current} configuration: a configuration may stand far from every limit and still be tens of increments from an unavoidable exit. The field that measures how long the exits can be deferred is the value function itself, and an estimate of it already exists wherever a reinforcement-learning baseline has been trained: the critic of an actor--critic run regresses precisely the discounted future progress of Eq.~(\ref{eq:value}) under its own improving policy. Reading that critic $\widehat{V}$ through the same finite-rate law gives the third member of the family,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:value-law}
\mathbf{a}_{V}(\mathbf{q})
= \arg\max_{\mathbf{a}\in\{-1,1\}^{m},\; F(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{a})\in\Omega}
\widehat{V}\bigl(F(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{a})\bigr),
\end{equation}
where $F(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{a})$ is the exact one-step flow of
Eq.~(\ref{eq:affine}) under the held command and the feasibility test
discards vertices whose successor leaves $\Omega$. This is the finite-rate
finite-difference reading of $\operatorname{sgn}(\mathbf{G}^{\!\top}\nabla
\widehat{V})$, and its form is that of one policy-improvement step computed
at deployment time: for the \emph{exact} value of a policy, the greedy
policy is guaranteed not to be worse than that policy. The deployed critic
is an approximation learned from finite data, so this guarantee motivates
the construction rather than certifies it; whether the approximate critic
is accurate enough is answered empirically in Section~\ref{sec:setup}. In
implementation the critic input is the full policy observation, i.e.,
$\widehat{V}\bigl(\mathbf{o}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{c},\mathbf{a})\bigr)$, where
the candidate command occupies the previous-action slot of the successor
observation.

Two properties make this consumption of a learned value reliable where a learned \emph{policy} is not. First, by Eq.~(\ref{eq:cl-vs-opt}) the optimal action \emph{is} a derivative of the value, and approximating a function well does not bound the error of its derivative; moreover, the critic receives a regression target at every state, while the policy gradient must extract the difference between the best and the second-best action from returns whose variance exceeds that difference by orders of magnitude. Second, Eq.~(\ref{eq:value-law}) never asks the network for a derivative: the exact model converts the action discrimination into a comparison of $2^{m}$ distinct states, in which the smooth approximation errors at neighbouring successors largely cancel. Section~\ref{sec:setup} tests every link of this argument.
}

\subsection{Task-Constrained Joint Trajectory Generation}
\label{sec:traj}
\new{This stage generates the trajectory incrementally, since neither the horizon nor the number of decision variables is known before the problem is solved: the quantity being maximized is the length at which the trajectory ends. At each increment the end-effector advances a fixed arc length, imposing Eq.~\eqref{eq:pos_constraint} increment by increment so that deviation never accumulates, and the redundancy is resolved in the null space of the position Jacobian. The only design choice is the null-space command $\mathbf{u}$.}



\new{At each increment, the $2^{m}$ vertices of the command box are
advanced one held command ahead by the exact model, infeasible successors
are discarded, and the survivors are scored by the deployed scalar field,
namely the margin field of Eq.~(\ref{eq:margin-law}) when no training is
available and the learned value field of Eq.~(\ref{eq:value-law}) when a
critic exists; the maximizer is executed. Both laws cost the same $2^{m}$
successor evaluations per increment. On the curved members of the path
family, the task term additionally carries a lateral feedback that holds
the tool inside the tolerance tube of Eq.~\eqref{eq:pos_constraint}; this
feedback is inert on straight rays.}

Every resolution law outputs an $m$-dimensional command $\mathbf{a}\in[-1,1]^{m}$ in a task-aligned null-space basis $\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{q})$, constructed by Gram--Schmidt inside the exact null space of the position Jacobian: the three projected objective gradients of Eq.~\eqref{eq:secondary} are orthonormalized first, and the orthogonal complement completes the basis. The command $\dot{\mathbf{q}}_{\mathrm{null}}=\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{q})\,(a_{\max}\mathbf{a})$ is added to the same task term for every law, so all laws \new{share one parameterization and are interchangeable at any increment}.

\new{\paragraph*{Learned baseline policies} The experiments compare the deployed laws against policies trained with Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) under identical conditions, over the continuous box and over its $2^{m}$ vertices with a categorical head.}
The observation exposes the normalized joint configuration and its square (making proximity to either limit directly visible), the task vectors $\mathbf{d}$ and $\mathbf{n}$, the tool axis with its cross and inner products with $\mathbf{n}$ (the orientation error relative to the cone), and the previous command; the end-effector position is excluded because deviation from the path is held inside the tolerance by the task term. \new{Their reward is the clipped per-step progress along $\mathbf{d}$, normalized by $v\,\Delta t$, with no terminal penalty, so the return approximates the survival time and the trained policy pursues exactly the objective of Eq.~(\ref{eq:value})}. \new{A supervised variant of the vertex policy hands the increment to the classical law inside a thin boundary shell on the normalized joint-limit distance, with hysteresis $(0.98,0.94)$ fixed by a sweep; because all laws share the basis and task term, the end-effector increment is unchanged at a switch.}

\new{\label{sec:division}The division of labor behind this design is supported by direct measurement. Learning resolves \emph{states}: a value network regressing stroke outcomes reaches $R^{2}$ above $0.9$, and candidate starts spread by hundreds of millimeters. Learning does not resolve \emph{actions}: the $2^{m}$ vertices differ in one-step margin only by $5\times10^{-3}$ to $2\times10^{-2}$, direct best-vertex classification attains $24.7\%$ held-out accuracy, and none of seven probe parameterizations ($\gamma$-swept and margin-shaped PPO $1.37$--$1.45$, fitted value iteration $1.12$--$1.25$, one-step argmax classification $0.93$) beats the free analytic gradient. The exact model closes exactly this gap by converting the action discrimination into a comparison of distinct states; the actor--critic decomposition of Section~\ref{sec:ladder} exhibits both halves inside one training run.}

\subsection{Initial Joint Configuration Selection}
\label{sec:select}
For many tasks the achievable path length is largely determined before motion begins: a fixed end-effector pose leaves a one-dimensional self-motion manifold whose branches share the pose yet differ sharply in reachable path length. \new{This structure, and its consequence for redundancy resolution, are long established: null-space techniques cannot leave the manifold they start on~\cite{burdick1989characterization}, and joint limits split the manifold into disconnected branches~\cite{luck1993self,shimizu2008analytical}. One clarification is needed here: the task pins only the tool position and constrains the orientation by an inequality, so the candidates span the connected components of the task-feasible configuration set at the starting point, of which fixed-pose self-motion-manifold branches are the fully constrained special case. We keep the shorter word \emph{branch} throughout. What this paper adds is not the structure but a measurement of its magnitude (Section~\ref{sec:setup}).}

Therefore, this selection module aims to provide \new{the trajectory generation stage} with an initial joint configuration within a favorable branch. \new{The candidate layer must supply coverage of the branches, not precision: the IKSel table~\cite{yuan2026iksel} is queried along $N_{\mathrm{dir}}$ cone directions, reranked by the full Jacobian metric, refined, and reduced to $K$ candidates by farthest-point selection. The achieved length saturates in branches reached and is insensitive to per-candidate refinement.}

\new{Which candidate to start from cannot be read off any instantaneous geometric quantity, because the consequence becomes visible only at the end of the stroke. The selector is therefore supervised by the outcome itself: every candidate of every training task is rolled out under the deployed law, and the stroke lengths serve as the regression targets. Relabeling for a new manipulator, path family, or deployed law amounts to one batch of rollouts.}

\new{The score is permutation-equivariant over the candidate set: each candidate is encoded together with a mean-pooled context of the others and trained with a listwise ranking loss. The score is further conditioned on the task through a \emph{road table}, the path ahead sampled at fixed arc lengths, which makes the architecture applicable to every path family of Section~III; whether the tested distributions require this input is examined in Section~\ref{sec:ood}. Deployment costs one table query, one forward pass, and one refinement; no candidate is rolled out at deployment time.}

\section{Experiments and Analysis}
\label{sec:setup}
\new{This section presents the experiments conducted to evaluate the proposed framework. First, we compare the resolution laws on $10{,}000$ tasks per manipulator. Second, we conduct ablation studies on the optimization horizon, the value source, and the action spacing. Third, we evaluate the initial-configuration selector and its generalization across path families. Finally, we analyze manipulator base placement and present real-world validation.}

\new{All experiments use the \textit{One} robotic system\footnote{https://github.com/wanweiwei07/one} on the 7-DoF xArm7, the 7-DoF Franka Research 3 (FR3) and the 6-DoF Cobotta, with a pen-shaped tool; the same candidate layer, selector and resolution laws run on all three, only the kinematic model changes. Shared settings: cone half-angle $30^{\circ}$, $K=8$ candidates from $N_{\mathrm{dir}}=32$ directions, and classical weights $(0.8,0.4,0.2)$.} The evaluation set \new{for each manipulator} comprises $10{,}000$ tasks, each specified by a starting position $\mathbf{p}_0$, a desired line direction $\mathbf{d}$, and a task-plane normal $\mathbf{n}$, sampled \new{within that manipulator's own work range, so the three sets are not in correspondence and only the realized fraction is comparable across them}. \new{The evaluation tasks are disjoint from those used to train the selector and the policy.} 

\new{Unless otherwise specified, experiments use the default setting: (1) a candidate pool of $K$ configurations retrieved from the IKSel table along $N_{\mathrm{dir}}$ directions inside the orientation cone, with the task-generating configuration as a fallback slot; (2) a road-table-conditioned selector that picks one candidate, followed by a single damped-least-squares refinement and no deployment-time rollout; (3) the one-step vertex law with temperature $\tau_{\mathrm{m}}=0.1$ for the margin field; the learned value field is the critic of the vertex-PPO baseline of Section~\ref{sec:traj} ($30$M steps, $1.75$\,h wall-clock on one GPU, trained once per manipulator). }

\new{A stroke must pass through every point of the path up to where it stops, so the first path sample at which no admissible configuration exists bounds the achievable length from above. Marching along each task's path and searching for an admissible configuration at each sample gives the \emph{pointwise-reachable length} $\ell^{\mathrm{pw}}$; the unknown true maximum stroke $\mathcal{L}^{*}$ then satisfies $\ell \le \mathcal{L}^{*} \le \ell^{\mathrm{pw}}$ for every executed stroke $\ell$. Failure of a numerical search does not certify that no configuration exists, so $\ell^{\mathrm{pw}}$ is a numerical estimate rather than a certified bound, and we treat it accordingly. First, the search is made \emph{witness-consistent}: configurations interpolated along the evaluated rollouts are injected as feasibility certificates at their own path samples, and the upper side is defined per task as the maximum of the search estimate and the executed stroke, so it is never below any evaluated stroke by construction. Without the witnesses, strong rollouts overtake the raw search on $11.8\%$ of tasks, by $8$\,mm on average and $63$\,mm at most; with them, the residual adjustment affects $7.0\%$ of tasks by at most $5$\,mm, a quarter of the march resolution. Second, the sensitivity to the search budget is quantified: raising the direction count from $24$ to $64$ raises the mean estimate by $12.7$\,mm and from $64$ to $128$ by a further $4.2$\,mm, while halving the march step from $20$ to $10$\,mm lowers it by $7.7$\,mm. Third, the resulting bracket is tight: over the $10{,}000$ FR3 tasks the executed stroke realizes a median $0.98$ of $\ell^{\mathrm{pw}}$ (mean $0.95$), so $\mathcal{L}^{*}$ is localized to within a few centimetres per task.}






\subsection{\new{Evaluation Protocol}}
\new{The controller comparison is paired per task (mean and median, bootstrap $95\%$ intervals), so its primary statistic is the per-task ratio to the classical law, which needs no reference length; differences within the two-training-run noise floor are reported as indistinguishable. The bracket enters where a per-task scale is needed, namely the pointwise-versus-continuous gap of Fig.~\ref{fig:pwgap} and the base-placement study. References that executed strokes are observed to exceed, such as a voxelized reachability chord and the best-of-$K$ rollouts of~\cite{yuan2026extreme}, are excluded by exactly this test.}

\subsubsection{\new{Action-Space Structure}}
\new{The shape of the learned command confirms the vertex reduction empirically. The classical command is an interior point of the box (median magnitude $0.14$ of the half-width), whereas the learned continuous policy saturates it (median $1.00$, with $89.2\%$ of steps on the boundary). Projecting the continuous command onto the nearest vertex changes the realized length by only $+0.004$ with a confidence interval spanning zero, indicating that the expressible magnitudes carry no information. In contrast, a policy trained natively over the $2^{4}$ vertices exceeds the continuous one by $+0.088$ with intervals excluding zero. The continuous learned resolution is therefore the discrete switching controller of Section~\ref{sec:hjb}, expressed through a parameterization (tanh saturation) that trains worse.}

{\color{red}
Beneath the numbers of Table~\ref{tab:mainresult}, the mechanism also replicates across the three manipulators. On every arm the classical law terminates overwhelmingly on the cone constraint ($81$--$85\%$), and the switching laws convert those endings with one shared strategy: they maintain the joint-limit margin early in the stroke and consume it near the end on whichever constraint is closest to violation. Moreover, the FR3-trained policy executed unchanged on the xArm7 retains about $80\%$ of its native gain.
}


\subsubsection{\new{Executability and Finite-Rate Consistency}}
\new{A switch alters only the null-space component of the increment, so the end-effector motion is unaffected and the joint-space discontinuity is bounded by $a_{\max}$; generated trajectories are time-parameterized with standard methods~\cite{pham2018toppra} and checked against manufacturer limits before replay.}


\new{Time parameterization repairs the trajectory after the fact; a second
test constrains the command as it is issued. A rate limiter
$|\mathbf{a}_{k}-\mathbf{a}_{k-1}|\le\Delta a_{\max}$ is applied at
deployment, and $\Delta a_{\max}$ is swept from $2$ (no restriction) down
to $0.125$, tracing the trade-off between stroke retained and acceleration. If the stroke
survives moderate rate limiting, the high-frequency vertex sequence is not
itself physically essential: the benefit of the law lies in which orthant
it selects, not in the hardware executing every switch.}






\new{Refining the integration step to $25$ and $12.5$\,ms changes absolute lengths by less than $2\%$ and leaves every comparison essentially unchanged. The projected-versus-continuous paired difference stays positive at $25$ and $50$\,ms control periods and turns negative only at $100$\,ms and beyond; the sign flips exactly where the theory predicts that intermediate magnitudes begin to substitute for switches the controller can no longer make. The one-step laws are defined at exactly the deployed control period.}

\new{The within-pool oracle costs $K$ rollouts per task, whereas the selector costs one forward pass and gives up the measured remainder, a trade-off that matters at scale, where the placement study sweeps thousands of base poses.}

\subsection{Generalization Ability}

\subsubsection{\new{Beyond the Straight Ray}}
\new{On arcs the resolution laws transfer by construction, since they read only the current configuration, while the task term acquires the lateral feedback of Section~\ref{sec:traj}. On serpentine seams, training the learned baselines on the family or observing curvature changes nothing beyond the two-run noise floor, whereas the action-space choice accounts for $+0.075$--$0.093$; this indicates that the resolution decides from the configuration's distance to the boundaries. On non-planar workpieces the cone axis rotates, and the margin field reads the cone margin against the local axis.}

\subsubsection{Manipulator Base Placement Analysis}
\label{sec:place}
\new{Deployment poses the inverse question: the path is given and the base pose must be chosen so it can be executed; waypoint-wise reachability admits poses from which no single stroke covers the path (Fig.~\ref{fig:pwgap}).}

\new{The framework supplies the missing test directly: for each candidate base pose, transform the required path into that base frame, run the two stages, and record the fraction of the required path one continuous stroke covers; a pose is admissible only when the fraction reaches one, and is then chosen inside the admissible set by any secondary criterion.}

\new{Two consequences follow. First, the admissible set obtained this way is contained in the reachability-map set but is not a shrunken copy of it, since what removes a pose is the branch structure rather than the distance to the workspace edge. Second, because the strokes run as far as the kinematics permit, a path that a conservative placement would split into several setups may be covered from a single base pose.}



\subsection{Real-World Validation}
\label{sec:real}
\new{Everything above is kinematic, so the physical trials are designed to establish executability rather than to re-measure the controller comparison: (i) the joint-limit and self-collision models agree with the hardware, (ii) the switching commands survive time parameterization, and (iii) the tool stays on the path under the manufacturer's tracking controller.}

\new{Trajectories are generated offline by the deployed system (selector followed by the value law), time-parameterized by TOPP-RA~\cite{pham2018toppra} under the manufacturer's velocity and acceleration limits, and replayed unmodified on a Franka Research 3 through its joint position interface; no learned component runs online. We select $[N]$ evaluation tasks stratified by the simulated stroke (short, medium, and long terciles) and replay, for each task, the margin-law trajectory, the value-law trajectory, and the rate-limited value-law variant of Section~\ref{sec:ladder}. Three quantities are recorded per trial. First, the \emph{executed fraction}, namely the stroke completed on hardware divided by the simulated stroke; a trial terminates when the controller reports a limit violation or the tracking error exceeds $\epsilon_{\mathrm{pos}}$. Second, the Cartesian deviation from the prescribed path (RMS and maximum), which measures whether the null-space switching disturbs the task-space motion after time parameterization. Third, the peak joint velocity and acceleration relative to the manufacturer limits, which quantifies how much dynamic headroom the kinematically generated trajectories consume.}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Conclusions and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\new{In this paper, we defined and quantified \textit{continuous kinematic reachability}, namely how much of a manipulator's pointwise reachability a single continuous stroke can realize. The quantity is bracketed per task between a numerical pointwise-reachability estimate and the executed stroke, across three manipulators and over a distribution of tasks.}

\new{The proposed framework rests on one principle, derived from the exit-time formulation: extreme motion generation requires long-horizon judgment delivered at fine action precision, and the two should be supplied by different instruments. The HJB analysis reduces redundancy resolution to evaluating a scalar potential with an exact one-step model, and the compared controllers order precisely by how much future information the potential carries. The critic of an ordinary reinforcement-learning run, read through the same law, achieves the best performance on all three manipulators at identical per-step cost, whereas the policy head of the same run stays below the training-free law. The same division of labor governs the choice of the initial configuration, through a selector supervised by rollouts of the deployed law.}

\new{Beyond the benchmark, the same measurement turns manipulator base placement into a task-aware decision. Future work will focus on extending the bracket to dynamic constraints and extending the road table to paths specified online.}
