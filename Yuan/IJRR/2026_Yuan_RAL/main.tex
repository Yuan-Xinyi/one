\begin{abstract}
This work studies \textit{extreme motion generation}, which aims to maximize the Cartesian path length that a manipulator executes along a prescribed path. Conventional reachability analysis indicates whether each point of a path is attainable. However, it does not indicate how far a \emph{single continuous joint trajectory} can carry the tool before a joint limit, an orientation tolerance, or a collision terminates the motion, and the two quantities differ substantially. We quantify the realizable part as \textit{continuous kinematic reachability} and bracket it per task between a numerical pointwise-reachability estimate and the executed stroke. \new{To realize a large fraction of the bracket, we formulate stroke maximization as a discounted exit-time optimal control problem and show that the pointwise Hamiltonian maximization is attained on the vertices of the null-space command box, so every deployed controller switches among vertex commands according to a scalar field of the configuration. A training-free constraint-margin field already matches receding-horizon optimization of its own objective, and the critic of a standard reinforcement-learning run further improves the executed stroke at identical per-step computation, whereas the policy head of the same run remains far below its own critic consumed in this way. The initial configuration is chosen by a learned selector supervised by rollouts of the deployed controller.} We evaluate the proposed framework on three manipulators with 10,000 path-following simulation tasks each, together with real-world validations, and further show that the same measurement provides a task-aware reference for manipulator base placement.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}




\IEEEPARstart{C}{onsider} a compact, fixed-base manipulator executing a coating or welding operation over a large work surface. Intuition would suggest that the farthest reachable point is determined by the reachable workspace. In practice, however, the motion is continuous, and one of the safety constraints, such as joint limits, tool-orientation tolerance, or collision, is violated long before the end-effector approaches the workspace boundary. Consequently, the achievable path length is decided by how long those boundaries can be deferred during execution. We refer to the problem of maximizing the Cartesian path length along a prescribed path as \textit{extreme motion generation}. This is particularly important in industrial automation, where the length of a single continuous motion directly limits the area a manipulator can process in one setup. Rather than scaling up the manipulator or repeatedly relocating the base, exploiting the extreme motion enlarges the effective working range\footnote{The project website and related videos of this paper can be found at \url{https://yuan-xinyi.github.io/extreme-motion-generation/}.}.

(a) Farthest distance with pointwise-valid configurations; (b) farthest distance achievable by a single continuous joint trajectory. The median achievable length drops from $0.71$ to $0.46\,\mathrm{m}$.}

\new{The outcome of an extended stroke is determined by two coupled choices that no reachability map captures. The first is made once, before the motion begins: which branch of the task-feasible configuration set the stroke starts on, a discrete choice no continuous motion can revise. The second is made at every control step: how the redundancy is resolved, since the joint motion chosen now steers which constraint the arm meets tens of increments later. The horizon itself, namely when the stroke ends, is exactly the quantity being optimized, so we formulate the problem as an \emph{exit-time} optimal control problem, whose objective is the feasible progress accumulated before the first constraint violation.}

\new{The formulation leads to one simple structure. All long-horizon knowledge about a task is summarized by a scalar \emph{value field} over configurations, namely the feasible progress still attainable from each configuration, and the best null-space command simply moves the configuration uphill on this field through the exact kinematic model. Reinforcement learning already estimates this field: the critic of an ordinary actor--critic run regresses exactly this quantity. Our key observation is that the two products of one training run behave very differently. The policy head, which must compress the uphill direction of the field into its own weights, performs poorly when deployed directly. The critic, consumed as the scalar field of a one-step lookahead over the exact model, performs best among all controllers we evaluate, at the same per-step cost. In short, learning reliably supplies the long-horizon value of states, while the exact model supplies the fine distinctions between actions, and the proposed framework assigns each part accordingly.}

\new{We evaluate the proposed framework on path-following tasks across three manipulators, measuring performance against the pointwise-reachable length of each task so that the gap between pointwise and continuous reachability is itself quantified. Ablation studies examine the optimization horizon, the source of the learned value, the action space, and the initial-configuration selection. The learned selector further generalizes to unseen path families, and the same measurement supports task-aware manipulator base placement.}

\new{In summary, the main contributions of this work are as follows.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \new{We introduce the perspective of continuous kinematic reachability. It casts extreme motion generation as initial branch selection plus in-branch redundancy resolution, rather than pointwise reachability.}
\item \new{Based on exit-time optimal control, we unify in-branch control as vertex selection driven by a scalar value field. The field is either a training-free constraint-margin field or a learned long-horizon critic.}
\item \new{Experiments on three manipulators with $10{,}000$ tasks each validate the framework across path families, action spaces, horizons, and selection rules. They reveal a division of labor: learning judges long-horizon states, and the exact model discriminates local actions.}
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{\new{Reachability and Global Redundancy Resolution}}
Manipulability characterizes local dexterity through the
Jacobian~\cite{yoshikawa1985manipulability,yoshikawa1985dynamic,chiu1988task,klein1987dexterity}
and has been folded into null-space
resolution~\cite{liegeois1977automatic,jin2017manipulability,jaquier2021geometry,haviland2021neo}
and planning~\cite{shen2023adaptive,cai2025just}; workspace reachability
characterizes global feasibility through capability
maps~\cite{yang2009placement,zacharias2007capturing} used for base
placement~\cite{makhal2018reuleaux,vahrenkamp2013robot} and learned
priors~\cite{feng2025predictive,jauhri2022robot,selim2022safe}. Neither
indicates how joint motion should be coordinated so that a single
continuous path approaches the reachable boundary. Closer to that
question, the global structure of the solution set is well understood:
redundant IK solutions form disjoint self-motion
manifolds~\cite{burdick1989inverse}, joint limits split them into
disconnected branches~\cite{shimizu2008analytical,luck1993self}, and
global resolution has been posed as continuous
pseudoinversion~\cite{hauser2018global} or dynamic programming across
homotopy classes~\cite{ferrentino2020optimal,ferrentino2021globally,fabregat2025topological};
extremal solutions are globally optimal within their own
class~\cite{martin1989resolution,suh1987local,kazerounian1988global}.
These works treat the Cartesian path as given and ask how to execute it
optimally. They do not ask how far the path can be extended, nor how much
of the achievable extent is decided by the branch as against the law
applied within it, measured over a distribution of tasks.

\subsection{\new{Continuous Path Feasibility and Maximum Executable Extent}}
\new{Two lines of work are closely related to this measurement, and we
state the differences explicitly. The first line asks whether a
prescribed path can be executed at all by one continuous joint
trajectory, including the hierarchy of pointwise, pathwise and global
resolution~\cite{hauser2016continuous}, paths whose waypoints are
individually reachable while no continuous resolution
exists~\cite{hauser2018global}, and the $t$-connected regions of the
cuspidal literature~\cite{wenger1991uniqueness,wenger2023cuspidal,elias2025cuspidal}.
These are set-valued or binary statements. The second line counts interruptions when a
path cannot be covered entirely, minimizing reconfigurations or
breakpoints~\cite{wang2024iklink,yang2022optimal,yin2024breakpoints}.
However, a count yields no arc length and no upper reference, and by
permitting reconfiguration it cannot ask how far a \emph{single} stroke
reaches. Closest in spirit, Gautier et al.~\cite{gautier2024comparison}
reported maximum weldable extents along prescribed trajectories, but as
case-study numbers for a non-redundant arm, which are one-sided by
construction. Therefore, a per-task, two-sided, distribution-level
measurement, together with the attribution of the measured extent to the
choices that produce it, is still missing; supplying it is one goal of
this paper.}

\subsection{\new{Optimal and Learned Redundancy Control}}
\new{When the objective is long-horizon rather than instantaneous,
redundancy resolution becomes an optimal control problem. In time-optimal
path following, control-affine dynamics with box-bounded inputs force the
optimal command to the boundary of the admissible set except on singular
arcs~\cite{bobrow1985time,shin1985minimum}; the vertex structure used in
this paper is the kinematic counterpart of that classical result.
Learning-based resolution~\cite{malik2022deep} and generative IK
solvers~\cite{ames2022ikflow,zhang2025ikdiffuser,huang2024diffusionseeder,yoon2023learning}
model the multiplicity of feasible configurations, and reinforcement
learning trains reactive policies that map states directly to null-space
commands. These methods deploy the learned \emph{policy}, while the value
function estimated by the same training is used only as a training
signal. In contrast, this paper deploys the learned \emph{value}: an
exact one-step model enumerates the vertex commands and the critic ranks
their successors, so that learning performs long-horizon state judgment
while the model performs local action discrimination.}

\section{Problem Formulation}
While extreme motion generation applies broadly to constrained path following, we here specify it as extreme straight-line path following. Specifically, a fixed-base manipulator advances its end-effector along the line with a tool-orientation constraint. Solving it requires choosing the initial joint configuration and then generating the motion that maximizes the achievable path length.



Formally, consider a manipulator with $n$ degrees of freedom, where $\mathbf{q} \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$ denotes the joint configuration. Let $\mathbf{p}(\mathbf{q}) \in \mathbb{R}^{3}$ denote the end-effector position and $\mathbf{z}(\mathbf{q}) \in \mathbb{R}^{3}$ denote the TCP local $z$-axis. A straight-line path-following task is specified by the condition vector $\mathbf{c} = [\mathbf{p}_{0}, \mathbf{d}, \mathbf{n}] \in \mathbb{R}^{9}$, where $\mathbf{p}_{0}$ is the starting position and $\mathbf{d}$, $\mathbf{n}$ are unit vectors giving the motion direction and task-plane normal. \new{The prescribed path is the ray $\mathcal{P}=\{\mathbf{p}_{0}+s\,\mathbf{d} : s\ge 0\}$, along which the end-effector advances at a commanded speed $v$, one command being issued per control period $\Delta t$.} \new{The straight ray extends directly to curved paths: a signed curvature bends it into an arc, a sinusoidal lateral offset produces a serpentine seam, and a slowly rotating cone axis $\mathbf{n}(s)$ models a non-planar workpiece. The formulation carries over unchanged by reading $\mathbf{d}$ as the instantaneous tangent and $\mathbf{n}$ as the local cone axis.}

Starting from an initial configuration $\mathbf{q}_{0}^{*}$, the policy $\pi$ chooses the joint motion at every control step, producing a joint trajectory that ends at step $T$, when a safety constraint is first violated. \new{The executed motion is a \emph{stroke} of Cartesian length $\mathcal{L}$.} The task is the constrained optimization problem:
\begin{align}
\max_{\mathbf{q}_{0}^{*},\,\pi} \quad & \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{q}^{*}_{0},\,\pi;\,\mathbf{p}_{0}, \mathbf{d}, \mathbf{n}) \\
\text{s.t.} \quad & {\color{red}\operatorname{dist}\bigl(\mathbf{p}(\mathbf{q}_{t}^{*}),\,\mathcal{P}\bigr) \leq \epsilon_{\text{pos}}}, \label{eq:pos_constraint} \\
& \arccos\!\left( \mathbf{z}(\mathbf{q}_{t}^{*})^{\top} \mathbf{n} \right) \leq \theta_{\max}, \label{eq:ori_constraint} \\
& \mathbf{q}_{\min} \preceq \mathbf{q}_{t}^{*} \preceq \mathbf{q}_{\max}, \label{eq:jl_constraint}
\end{align}
where $\mathcal{L}$ denotes the stroke length, $\mathbf{q}_0^*$ and $\pi$ are the initial configuration and the control policy to be optimized, and $(\mathbf{p}_0, \mathbf{d}, \mathbf{n})$ specify the task conditions as above. The $\epsilon_{\text{pos}}$, $\theta_{\max}$, and $[\mathbf{q}_{\min}, \mathbf{q}_{\max}]$ are the position tolerance, orientation tolerance, and joint limits, respectively.

Since only the $3$-DoF position must be tracked, the manipulator is kinematically redundant for $n>3$, and the commanded joint velocity decomposes into a task term and a null-space term,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:nullspace}
{\color{red}
\dot{\mathbf{q}} = \mathbf{J}^{+}_{\lambda}\dot{\mathbf{x}} + \mathbf{B}(\mathbf{q})\,\mathbf{u},
\qquad
\mathbf{J}\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{0},\quad
\mathbf{B}^{\!\top}\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{I},}
\end{equation}
\new{where $\mathbf{J}^{+}_{\lambda}$ is the damped pseudo-inverse of the
position Jacobian and $\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{q})$ an orthonormal basis of its
\emph{exact} null space (double-precision SVD; task-aligned construction in
Section~\ref{sec:traj}), so $\mathbf{J}\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{0}$ holds by
construction and the secondary motion cannot disturb the end-effector
regardless of the damping; $\mathbf{u}$ collects the null-space coordinates.}
Within this null space the classical law performs gradient ascent on a
weighted secondary field,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:secondary}
\boldsymbol{\xi}=k_{\mu}\,\nabla_{\mathbf{q}}w_{\mathbf{d}}(\mathbf{q})\;-\;k_{\mathrm{jl}}\,\frac{\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{q}_{\mathrm{mid}}}{\mathbf{q}_{\max}-\mathbf{q}_{\min}}\;+\;k_{\theta}\,g(\theta)\,\nabla_{\mathbf{q}}\!\big(\mathbf{z}(\mathbf{q})^{\top}\mathbf{n}\big),
\end{equation}
where the secondary field $\boldsymbol{\xi}$ combines a directional-manipulability term $w_{\mathbf{d}}$ that retreats from singularities along $\mathbf{d}$, a centering term that pulls each joint toward the middle $\mathbf{q}_{\mathrm{mid}}$ of its range, and a cone term gated by $g(\theta)$ that activates only as the tool axis approaches the $\theta_{\max}$ boundary. \new{It enters the decomposition as $\mathbf{u}\propto\mathbf{B}^{\!\top}\boldsymbol{\xi}$; all controllers share Eq.~(\ref{eq:nullspace}) and differ only in how $\mathbf{u}$ is generated.}

\new{Throughout this work the null-space command is bounded,
$\mathbf{u} = a_{\max}\,\mathbf{a}$ with
$\mathbf{a} \in \mathcal{A} := [-1,1]^{m}$, where $m = n-3$ is the
null-space dimension left by the position task. Under this bound the
decomposition of Eq.~(\ref{eq:nullspace}) is control-affine,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:affine}
\dot{\mathbf{q}} \;=\; \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{q}) \;+\; \mathbf{G}(\mathbf{q})\,\mathbf{a},
\qquad
\mathbf{f} := \mathbf{J}^{+}_{\lambda}\dot{\mathbf{x}},\quad
\mathbf{G} := a_{\max}\,\mathbf{B},
\end{equation}
where the task condition $\mathbf{c}$ is a fixed parameter of
$\mathbf{f}$.}

\section{\texorpdfstring{\new{Extreme Motion Generation}}{Extreme Motion Generation}}
\label{sec:hjb}

\new{Extending the stroke depends on two decisions. Before the motion
begins, the initial joint configuration fixes the branch on which the
whole stroke runs; during the motion, the redundancy must be resolved at
every increment. This section first casts the shared objective as an
exit-time optimal control problem, then presents the two stages in
execution order: initial joint configuration selection, and value-guided
motion generation. Both stages follow one principle: candidates are
generated and screened by the exact kinematic model, while a learned
network provides the long-horizon judgment among them.}

{\color{red}
\subsection{Exit-Time Optimal Control Formulation}
The objective of Section~III is the stroke length, and the stroke ends at
the first constraint violation, so the horizon is not given in advance:
it is decided by the very commands being optimized. To make this
well-posed, collect the constraints of
Eqs.~(\ref{eq:pos_constraint})--(\ref{eq:jl_constraint}), together with
the self-collision clearance, into an open feasible set $\Omega$ of
configurations, and let
$\tau_{\mathrm{exit}} = \inf\{t \ge 0 : \mathbf{q}(t) \notin \Omega\}$
denote the exit time of a closed-loop trajectory. Writing
$r(\mathbf{q}) \ge 0$ for the instantaneous advance rate along
$\mathbf{d}$, define the value
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:value}
V(\mathbf{q}_{0}) \;=\; \sup_{\mathbf{a}(\cdot)}
\int_{0}^{\tau_{\mathrm{exit}}} e^{-\beta t}\, r(\mathbf{q}(t))\,\mathrm{d}t,
\end{equation}
where the supremum runs over measurable commands into $\mathcal{A}$. The
discount $\beta>0$ keeps the objective well defined when the horizon is
unbounded and matches the reinforcement-learning components introduced
below. On the straight-line family the task term holds the advance rate
at $v$, so $V$ increases with the exit time alone: maximizing $V$ means
deferring the exit. $V$ in (\ref{eq:value}) is the optimal value; the
learned components of this paper approximate values of \emph{policies}.
The formulation states precisely how a decision must be judged: by how it
changes all future feasible progress through $V$, not by any quantity
evaluable at the current configuration in isolation.

Both stages of the framework are built on this one quantity. Initial
configuration selection needs the value of candidate \emph{start} states,
and motion generation needs, at every increment, the value of candidate
\emph{successor} states. In both cases the exact model generates and
screens the candidates, and a learned estimate of $V$ judges them.
}

\subsection{Initial Joint Configuration Selection}
\label{sec:select}
For many tasks the achievable path length is largely determined before motion begins: a fixed end-effector pose leaves a one-dimensional self-motion manifold whose branches share the pose yet differ sharply in reachable path length. \new{This structure, and its consequence for redundancy resolution, are long established: null-space techniques cannot leave the manifold they start on~\cite{burdick1989characterization}, and joint limits split the manifold into disconnected branches~\cite{luck1993self,shimizu2008analytical}. One clarification is needed here: the task pins only the tool position and constrains the orientation by an inequality, so the candidates span the connected components of the task-feasible configuration set at the starting point, of which fixed-pose self-motion-manifold branches are the fully constrained special case. We keep the shorter word \emph{branch} throughout.}

Therefore, this selection module aims to provide \new{the motion generation stage} with an initial joint configuration within a favorable branch. \new{The candidate layer must supply coverage of the branches, not precision: the IKSel table~\cite{yuan2026iksel} is queried along $N_{\mathrm{dir}}$ cone directions, reranked by the full Jacobian metric, refined, and reduced to $K$ candidates by farthest-point selection.}

\new{Which candidate to start from cannot be read off any instantaneous geometric quantity, because the consequence becomes visible only at the end of the stroke. The selector is therefore supervised by the outcome itself: every candidate of every training task is rolled out under the deployed controller of Section~\ref{sec:traj}, and the stroke lengths serve as the regression targets. Relabeling for a new manipulator, path family, or deployed controller amounts to one batch of rollouts.}

\new{The selector scores the candidate set in a permutation-equivariant way: each candidate is encoded together with a mean-pooled context of the others and trained with a listwise ranking loss. The score is further conditioned on the task through a \emph{road table}, the path ahead sampled at fixed arc lengths, which makes the architecture applicable to every path family of Section~III; whether the tested distributions require this input is examined in Section~\ref{sec:ood}. Deployment costs one table query, one forward pass, and one refinement; no candidate is rolled out at deployment time.}

\subsection{\new{Value-Guided Motion Generation}}
\label{sec:traj}
\new{This stage generates the trajectory incrementally, since neither the horizon nor the number of decision variables is known before the problem is solved: the quantity being maximized is the length at which the trajectory ends. At each increment the end-effector advances a fixed arc length, imposing Eq.~\eqref{eq:pos_constraint} increment by increment so that deviation never accumulates, and the redundancy is resolved in the null space of the position Jacobian.}

\new{The command $\mathbf{a}$ acts in the task-aligned null-space basis $\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{q})$, constructed by Gram--Schmidt inside the exact null space of the position Jacobian: the three projected objective gradients of Eq.~\eqref{eq:secondary} are orthonormalized first, and the orthogonal complement completes the basis. The command $\dot{\mathbf{q}}_{\mathrm{null}}=\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{q})\,(a_{\max}\mathbf{a})$ is added to the same task term for every controller, so all compared controllers share one parameterization and are interchangeable at any increment.}

{\color{red}
The remaining design choice is how to pick $\mathbf{a}$, and the HJB
equation, the continuous-time form of Bellman's optimality principle,
turns the value field into a feedback rule for exactly this choice. At
points where $V$ is differentiable, the discounted exit-time problem
satisfies
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:hjb}
\beta V \;=\; r \;+\; \nabla V^{\!\top}\mathbf{f}
\;+\; \sup_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{A}}
\bigl(\mathbf{G}^{\!\top}\nabla V\bigr)^{\!\top}\mathbf{a},
\qquad V \equiv 0 \ \text{outside } \Omega .
\end{equation}
Since $r$ and $\mathbf{f}$ are independent of $\mathbf{a}$, the command
appears only in the last term and enters it linearly. Define the
\emph{switching vector}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sigma}
\boldsymbol{\sigma}(\mathbf{q}) \;:=\;
\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{q})^{\!\top}\nabla V(\mathbf{q}) \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{m},
\end{equation}
whose $i$-th component measures how motion along the $i$-th null-space
direction changes discounted future feasible progress. The supremum in
(\ref{eq:hjb}) is therefore $\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}\|_{1}$ and, whenever no
component vanishes, is attained at the vertex
$\mathbf{a}=\operatorname{sgn}\boldsymbol{\sigma}$. Thus the value-based
resolution selects the orthant of the null-space command rather than its
magnitude.

This vertex structure is not specific to the optimal value $V$. The same
argument applies to the gradient of any differentiable scalar field, allowing
the feedback form to be used with an approximate value estimate.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:vertex}
Let $W:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}$ be differentiable and
$\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{W} := \mathbf{G}^{\!\top}\nabla W$. Then, at every
$\mathbf{q}$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:prop}
\max_{\mathbf{a}\in[-1,1]^{m}} \boldsymbol{\sigma}_{W}^{\!\top}\mathbf{a}
\;=\;
\max_{\mathbf{a}\in\{-1,1\}^{m}} \boldsymbol{\sigma}_{W}^{\!\top}\mathbf{a}
\;=\; \|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{W}\|_{1},
\end{equation}
attained at $\mathbf{a}=\operatorname{sgn}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{W}$, with the
components on which $\sigma_{W,i}=0$ chosen arbitrarily in $\{-1,1\}$.
Consequently, a greedy policy with respect to any differentiable value
estimate can be chosen vertex-valued: the vertex action space is closed under
policy improvement.
\end{proposition}

\begin{IEEEproof}
$\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{W}^{\!\top}\mathbf{a}
= \sum_{i}\sigma_{W,i}\,a_{i} \le \sum_{i}|\sigma_{W,i}|$ for
$\mathbf{a}\in[-1,1]^{m}$, with equality iff
$a_{i}=\operatorname{sgn}\sigma_{W,i}$ on every component with
$\sigma_{W,i}\neq 0$; such an $\mathbf{a}$ can be chosen in
$\{-1,1\}^{m}$.
\end{IEEEproof}

\begin{remark}[Scope]
\label{rem:bangbang}
Three qualifications delimit this result. First, the box- and
vertex-constrained problems share the same pointwise Hamiltonian by
(\ref{eq:prop}); equality of their value functions would require viscosity
comparison conditions that we neither verify nor use. Second, when a component
of $\boldsymbol{\sigma}$ vanishes, an optimal vertex-valued selector exists,
rather than every optimal command being a vertex. Third, the reduction is
exact in continuous time; its finite-rate consistency is examined empirically
in Section~\ref{sec:setup}, while the command box itself remains a design
choice.
\end{remark}

The scalar field consumed by this feedback is estimated with an ordinary
actor--critic run. Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) trains a stochastic
policy $\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{a}\mid\mathbf{o})$ over the $2^{m}$ vertex
commands and a critic $\widehat{V}_{\!\phi}(\mathbf{o})$ on the same
rollouts. The observation $\mathbf{o}$ exposes the normalized joint
configuration and its square, the task vectors $\mathbf{d}$ and
$\mathbf{n}$, the tool axis with its inner and cross products with
$\mathbf{n}$, and the previous command; the end-effector position is
excluded because the task term holds the deviation inside the tolerance.
The reward is the clipped per-step progress along the path,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reward}
r_{t} \;=\; \operatorname{clip}\!\Bigl(
\tfrac{(\mathbf{p}_{t+1}-\mathbf{p}_{t})^{\!\top}\mathbf{d}}{v\,\Delta t},
\,0,\,1\Bigr),
\end{equation}
with no terminal penalty, so the return approximates the survival time and
the pair $(\pi_{\theta},\widehat{V}_{\!\phi})$ pursues exactly the
objective of Eq.~(\ref{eq:value}): the critic is trained by regression on
the discounted returns of the improving policy, i.e., it is an estimate of
the value field.

Deploying $\pi_{\theta}$ directly is the standard choice, and it serves as
a baseline in Section~\ref{sec:setup}. The proposed controller instead
consumes the \emph{critic} through the vertex structure derived above.
Reading the critic $\widehat{V}$ through the finite-rate form of the
vertex structure gives the deployed controller,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:value-law}
\mathbf{a}_{V}(\mathbf{q})
= \arg\max_{\mathbf{a}\in\{-1,1\}^{m},\; F(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{a})\in\Omega}
\widehat{V}\bigl(F(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{a})\bigr),
\end{equation}
where $F(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{a})$ is the exact one-step flow of
Eq.~(\ref{eq:affine}) under the held command and the feasibility test
discards vertices whose successor leaves $\Omega$. This is the finite-rate
finite-difference reading of $\operatorname{sgn}(\mathbf{G}^{\!\top}\nabla
\widehat{V})$, and its form is that of one policy-improvement step computed
at deployment time: for the \emph{exact} value of a policy, the greedy
policy is guaranteed not to be worse than that policy. The deployed critic
is an approximation learned from finite data, so this guarantee motivates
the construction rather than certifies it; whether the approximate critic
is accurate enough is answered empirically in Section~\ref{sec:setup}. In
implementation the critic input is the full policy observation, i.e.,
$\widehat{V}\bigl(\mathbf{o}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{c},\mathbf{a})\bigr)$, where
the candidate command occupies the previous-action slot of the successor
observation.

Two properties make this consumption of a learned value reliable where a learned \emph{policy} is not. First, by Eq.~(\ref{eq:sigma}) the optimal action \emph{is} the sign of a derivative of the value, and approximating a function well does not bound the error of its derivative; moreover, the critic receives a regression target at every state, while the policy gradient must extract the difference between the best and the second-best action from returns whose variance exceeds that difference by orders of magnitude. Second, Eq.~(\ref{eq:value-law}) never asks the network for a derivative: the exact model converts the action discrimination into a comparison of $2^{m}$ distinct states, in which the smooth approximation errors at neighbouring successors largely cancel. Section~\ref{sec:setup} tests every link of this argument.

The division of labor behind this design is supported by direct
measurement.\label{sec:division} Learning resolves \emph{states}: a value
network regressing stroke outcomes reaches $R^{2}$ above $0.9$, and
candidate starts spread by hundreds of millimeters. Learning does not
resolve \emph{actions}: the $2^{m}$ vertices differ in one-step margin
only by $5\times10^{-3}$ to $2\times10^{-2}$, direct best-vertex
classification attains $24.7\%$ held-out accuracy, and none of seven probe
parameterizations ($\gamma$-swept and margin-shaped PPO $1.37$--$1.45$,
fitted value iteration $1.12$--$1.25$, one-step argmax classification
$0.93$) beats the free analytic gradient. The exact model closes exactly
this gap by converting the action discrimination into a comparison of
distinct states; the actor--critic decomposition of
Section~\ref{sec:ladder} exhibits both halves inside one training run.

The same one-step skeleton also accepts instantaneous scalar fields: the
classical secondary objective of Eq.~(\ref{eq:secondary}) and an analytic
constraint-margin field instantiate it without any training. They serve as
baselines and are specified in Section~\ref{sec:setup}. On the curved
members of the path family, the task term additionally carries a lateral
feedback that holds the tool inside the tolerance tube of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:pos_constraint}; this feedback is inert on straight rays.
}

\section{Experiments and Analysis}
\label{sec:setup}
\subsection{Experimental Setup}

Experiments are conducted on the 7-DoF xArm7, the 7-DoF Franka Research 3
(FR3), and the 6-DoF Cobotta using the \textit{One} robotic
system\footnote{https://github.com/wanweiwei07/one}. Each manipulator is
evaluated on $10{,}000$ held-out tasks specified by the starting position
$\mathbf{p}_0$, path direction $\mathbf{d}$, and task-plane normal
$\mathbf{n}$. Training and evaluation tasks are disjoint.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1em]
\renewcommand{\labelitemi}{$\triangleright$}

    \item \uline{Task constraints:}
    Unless otherwise specified, the orientation-cone half-angle is
    $30^{\circ}$, and all controllers share the same task-space tracking,
    joint-limit, orientation, and collision constraints.

    \item \uline{Initial-configuration selection:}
    The candidate pool contains $K=8$ configurations retrieved from
    $N_{\mathrm{dir}}=32$ directions using IKSel. The learned selector chooses
    one candidate, followed by a single damped-least-squares refinement.

    \item \uline{Classical controller:}
    The classical null-space controller uses weights $(0.8,0.4,0.2)$.

    \item \uline{Margin controller:}
    The analytic margin field uses temperature $\tau_{\mathrm{m}}=0.1$.

    \item \uline{Learned value controller:}
    The value law uses the critic of the vertex-PPO baseline, trained for
    $30$M environment steps separately for each manipulator. All resolution
    laws share the same null-space parameterization and task term.

    \item \uline{Evaluation metrics:}
    Controller comparisons are paired per task and reported using mean,
    median, and bootstrap $95\%$ confidence intervals. The primary normalized
    metric is the per-task ratio to the classical controller. The
    pointwise-reachable length $\ell^{\mathrm{pw}}$ is additionally used where
    a task-specific reachability reference is required.

\end{itemize}

\new{Four path families are evaluated, as illustrated in
Fig.~\ref{fig:families}: straight rays and arcs appear in the training
distribution, whereas serpentine seams and rotating-axis paths are
excluded from training and serve as out-of-distribution evaluation.}

\subsection{\new{Baselines Comparison}}
\label{sec:ladder}

\new{We compare the proposed value-based resolution law against five baselines that
represent increasingly stronger alternatives for redundancy resolution. All
methods share the same task-space controller, constraints, initial
configuration, null-space parameterization, and control period.}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1em]
\renewcommand{\labelitemi}{$\triangleright$}

    \item \new{\uline{Zero Null-Space (Zero):}
    No secondary motion is applied, i.e.,
    $\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{0}$. This baseline measures the stroke provided by
    task-space tracking alone.}

    \item \new{\uline{Classical Gradient Resolution (Classical):}
    The conventional null-space controller of Eq.~\ref{eq:secondary}. It
    reads $\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{cl}} =
    \operatorname{clip}(\mathbf{G}^{\!\top}\nabla\Phi)$, the magnitude of
    an instantaneous hand-weighted surrogate $\Phi$, whereas the optimal
    form of Section~IV reads
    $\operatorname{sgn}(\mathbf{G}^{\!\top}\nabla V)$.}

    \item \new{\uline{Analytic Margin Resolution (Margin):}
    The same one-step vertex-selection structure as the proposed method,
    but successors are scored by the training-free constraint-margin field
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:margin-field}
    \Phi_{\mathrm{m}}(\mathbf{q})
    = -\tau_{\mathrm{m}} \log \sum_{j}
    \exp\bigl(-m_{j}(\mathbf{q})/\tau_{\mathrm{m}}\bigr),
    \end{equation}
    a soft minimum over the normalized joint-limit and orientation-cone
    margins $m_{j}$, i.e., the constraints whose exhaustion terminates
    strokes. It carries no learned long-horizon information.}

    \item \new{\uline{Reactive Vertex Policy (PPO):}
    The vertex-valued PPO policy of Section~\ref{sec:traj}, trained to
    output the null-space action directly from the current observation.}

    \item \new{\uline{Hybrid Reactive Policy (PPO+Classical):}
    The same PPO policy with the classical controller taking over inside a
    thin boundary shell on the normalized joint-limit distance (hysteresis
    $0.98/0.94$, fixed by a sweep).}

    \item \new{\uline{Critic-Guided Value Resolution (Value, Ours):}
    The proposed law of Eq.~\ref{eq:value-law}. It uses the critic learned
    from the same PPO run to score the exact one-step successor states and
    selects the highest-valued feasible vertex.}
\end{itemize}

\new{Table~\ref{tab:mainresult} reports the results on $10{,}000$ tasks per
manipulator. All rows share the task-generating initial configuration, so
this comparison isolates the resolution law from the selection stage; the
full framework with the learned selector is reported in
Table~\ref{tab:framework}. The same ordering is observed on all three
robots. Classical gradient resolution provides only a small improvement
over zero null-space motion, whereas the analytic margin baseline produces
a substantially longer stroke. Directly deploying the learned PPO policy
remains below the margin baseline on the two 7-DoF manipulators. In
contrast, the proposed value law achieves the best performance on all
three manipulators.}

\new{The comparison between PPO and Value is particularly informative because both
use the same actor--critic training run. Their performance difference
therefore arises from how the learned information is used: PPO maps the state
directly to an action, whereas Value uses the critic for long-horizon state
evaluation and the exact model for local action selection.}

\subsubsection{Manipulator Base Placement}
\label{sec:place}

Continuous kinematic reachability also provides a task-aware criterion for
manipulator base placement. For each candidate base pose, the required path is
transformed into the robot frame and evaluated by the proposed framework. A
base pose is considered feasible when the complete path can be covered by a
single continuous stroke.

Unlike waypoint-wise reachability, this criterion accounts for the continuity
of the joint trajectory and the initial configuration branch. It therefore
identifies base poses from which the required path is continuously executable,
rather than merely pointwise reachable.



\subsection{Real-World Validation}
\label{sec:real}

The proposed framework is kinematic, so the physical experiments evaluate
whether the generated extreme motions remain executable on hardware.
Trajectories are generated offline by the selector and value law,
time-parameterized using Ruckig~\cite{berscheid2021jerk} under the manufacturer's
joint velocity and acceleration limits, and replayed unmodified on a Franka
Research 3 through its joint-position interface.

We evaluate $[N]$ tasks spanning short to long simulated strokes and compare
the simulated and hardware executions in terms of completed stroke and
Cartesian path-tracking error. The hardware reproduces $[XX]\%$ of the
simulated stroke on average, with an RMS / maximum path deviation of
$[XX]/[XX]$\,mm. These results confirm that the kinematically generated
extreme motions can be transferred to the physical robot while preserving
the prescribed Cartesian path.


%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Conclusions and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
In this paper, we introduced \textit{continuous kinematic reachability} to
measure how much of a manipulator's pointwise reachable path can be realized
by a single continuous stroke. We evaluated this quantity across three
manipulators and a distribution of tasks using the executed stroke and a
numerical pointwise-reachability reference.

The proposed framework follows a simple principle: learning provides
long-horizon state evaluation, while the exact model resolves fine local
action differences. The exit-time formulation and HJB analysis lead to a
vertex-valued redundancy-resolution law, through which the learned critic
consistently outperforms both the corresponding policy head and the
training-free baselines. Initial configuration selection follows the same
principle by learning to rank candidate branches from rollout outcomes.

The same framework also provides a task-aware criterion for manipulator base
placement. Future work will extend the formulation to dynamic constraints and
paths specified online.
