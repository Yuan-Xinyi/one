\begin{abstract}
This work studies \textit{extreme motion generation}, which aims to maximize the Cartesian path length that a manipulator executes along a prescribed path. Conventional reachability analysis indicates whether individual path points are attainable, but not how far a \emph{single continuous joint trajectory} can carry the tool before joint limits, orientation tolerance, or collision terminate the motion. We refer to this maximum continuously executable extent as \textit{continuous kinematic reachability} and use numerical pointwise reachability as a task-specific reference for the realized stroke. Achieving a long stroke requires two coupled decisions: selecting the initial configuration and resolving redundancy during execution. We address both through candidate generation and learned ranking. Feasible initial configurations are ranked by a rollout-supervised selector. For motion generation, we formulate stroke maximization as a discounted exit-time optimal control problem and show that pointwise Hamiltonian maximization admits vertex-valued null-space commands. A kinematic one-step model generates feasible successor states under these commands, which are ranked by a learned critic. Across three manipulators and 10,000 simulation tasks per manipulator, the critic-guided controller consistently outperforms the corresponding policy head and training-free baselines, while learned initial selection further increases the achievable stroke. Real-world validation assesses physical executability, and the same continuous-path criterion extends naturally to task-aware manipulator base placement.



\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\IEEEPARstart{C}{onsider} a compact, fixed-base manipulator executing coating or welding over a large work surface. Reachable workspace indicates where the end-effector can be placed, but not how far it can move along a prescribed path through a \emph{single continuous joint trajectory}. In practice, joint limits, tool-orientation tolerance, or collision may terminate the motion well before the workspace boundary is reached. We refer to the problem of maximizing this continuous Cartesian path length as \textit{extreme motion generation}, and to the maximum continuously executable extent as \textit{continuous kinematic reachability}. This distinction is particularly relevant in industrial automation, where a longer continuous stroke directly enlarges the effective working range without scaling up the manipulator or repeatedly relocating its base\footnote{The project website and related videos of this paper can be found at \url{https://yuan-xinyi.github.io/extreme-motion-generation/}.}.

\label{fig:pwgap}
\end{figure*}

The achievable stroke depends on two coupled decisions that pointwise reachability does not capture. The first is the initial task-feasible configuration, which determines where the continuous motion begins within the feasible solution set. The second is the redundancy-resolution law applied throughout execution, which determines how quickly joint-limit, orientation, and collision margins are consumed. Because the stroke terminates at the first constraint violation, its length is naturally formulated as an \emph{exit-time} optimal control objective.

We address these two decisions through a common candidate-and-rank structure. For initial selection, kinematically feasible configurations are generated and ranked by a learned selector supervised by rollout outcomes. For motion generation, the exit-time formulation yields a value-based redundancy-resolution structure: pointwise Hamiltonian maximization over the bounded null-space command admits a vertex-valued solution. A known kinematic model therefore generates feasible one-step successors under the vertex commands, which can be ranked by either an analytic constraint-margin field or a learned critic. In particular, using the critic from the same actor--critic training run to rank successor states substantially outperforms deploying its policy head directly. This comparison suggests a useful division of labor: learning provides long-horizon state evaluation, while the kinematic model performs local action discrimination.

We evaluate the framework on three manipulators with $10{,}000$ held-out path-following tasks each. The experiments compare redundancy-resolution laws, including continuous- and vertex-action reactive policies, and initial-configuration selection rules. The learned selector is further evaluated on path families absent from its training distribution. Hardware experiments assess whether the generated motions remain executable after time parameterization, and the same continuous-path criterion naturally induces a task-aware criterion for manipulator base placement.

In summary, the main contributions of this work are as follows.
\begin{itemize}
\item We introduce \textit{continuous kinematic reachability} as the maximum path extent realizable by a single continuous joint trajectory, and cast extreme motion generation as the coupled problems of initial-configuration selection and redundancy resolution.

\item We formulate in-motion redundancy resolution as an exit-time optimal control problem and show that its pointwise Hamiltonian maximization admits vertex-valued null-space commands, enabling model-generated successors to be ranked by an analytic field or a learned critic.

\item Experiments on three manipulators show that critic-guided resolution consistently outperforms the corresponding policy head and training-free baselines, while learned initial selection further improves the achievable stroke and transfers to unseen path families.
\end{itemize}

The rest of this article is organized as follows. Section~II reviews related work. Section~III formulates the problem. Section~IV presents the framework. Section~V reports the experiments, and Section~VI concludes the paper.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Reachability and Global Redundancy Resolution}

Manipulability characterizes local dexterity through the
Jacobian~\cite{yoshikawa1985manipulability,yoshikawa1985dynamic,chiu1988task,klein1987dexterity}
and has been incorporated into null-space
resolution~\cite{liegeois1977automatic,jin2017manipulability,jaquier2021geometry,haviland2021neo}
and planning~\cite{shen2023adaptive,cai2025just}. Workspace reachability
instead characterizes global feasibility through capability
maps~\cite{yang2009placement,zacharias2007capturing}, with applications to
base placement~\cite{makhal2018reuleaux,vahrenkamp2013robot} and learned
reachability priors~\cite{feng2025predictive,jauhri2022robot,selim2022safe}.
Neither directly determines how far a prescribed path can be followed by
one continuous joint trajectory.

The global structure underlying this problem has been studied extensively.
Redundant IK solutions form self-motion
manifolds~\cite{burdick1989inverse}, joint limits can divide feasible
solutions into disconnected branches~\cite{shimizu2008analytical,luck1993self},
and global redundancy resolution has been formulated through continuous
pseudoinversion~\cite{hauser2018global} and dynamic programming across
solution classes~\cite{ferrentino2020optimal,ferrentino2021globally,fabregat2025topological}.
Related studies distinguish local and globally optimal redundancy
resolution~\cite{martin1989resolution,suh1987local,kazerounian1988global}.
These methods optimize how a prescribed path is executed, whereas we ask
how far the path remains continuously executable and how this extent
depends on the initial configuration and the subsequent redundancy-resolution
law.

\subsection{Continuous Path Feasibility and Maximum Executable Extent}

Prior work has also studied whether a prescribed path admits a continuous
joint-space realization. This includes the distinction between pointwise,
pathwise, and global resolution~\cite{hauser2016continuous}, cases in
which individually reachable waypoints admit no continuous global
resolution~\cite{hauser2018global}, and connected feasible regions in the
cuspidal-manipulator literature~\cite{wenger1991uniqueness,wenger2023cuspidal,elias2025cuspidal}.
These works primarily characterize feasibility or connectivity rather than
the maximum continuously executable path extent.

A related line of work minimizes reconfigurations or breakpoints when a
complete path cannot be executed continuously~\cite{wang2024iklink,yang2022optimal,yin2024breakpoints}.
Such formulations allow interrupted execution and therefore address a
different objective from maximizing the length of a \emph{single}
continuous stroke. Closest in spirit, Gautier et
al.~\cite{gautier2024comparison} reported maximum weldable extents along
prescribed trajectories for robot morphology and base-placement analysis.
Our focus is instead a task-level characterization of continuous
executable extent relative to pointwise reachability, together with the
roles of initial-configuration selection and redundancy resolution in
realizing that extent.

\subsection{Optimal and Learned Redundancy Control}

When redundancy resolution is judged by future feasible progress rather
than an instantaneous secondary objective, it naturally admits an optimal
control formulation. Classical time-optimal path-following methods show
that control-affine systems with bounded inputs often yield
boundary-valued optimal controls, except on singular
arcs~\cite{bobrow1985time,shin1985minimum}. Our exit-time formulation
leads to an analogous structure in null-space control: pointwise
Hamiltonian maximization over a box-bounded command admits a
vertex-valued solution.

Learning has also been used for redundancy resolution and inverse
kinematics~\cite{malik2022deep}, while generative and learned IK methods
model or exploit the multiplicity of feasible
configurations~\cite{ames2022ikflow,zhang2025ikdiffuser,huang2024diffusionseeder,yoon2023learning}.
In reinforcement-learning approaches, the policy is normally the object
deployed for action selection. In contrast, we use the critic learned in
the same actor--critic run to evaluate model-generated successor states.
The kinematic model generates and screens candidate vertex commands,
while the critic supplies long-horizon state evaluation. This separation
allows the learned value and the corresponding reactive policy to be
compared directly within the same training run.


\section{Problem Formulation}

We formulate extreme motion generation as constrained path following with an unknown terminal path extent. We first consider a straight-line task, in which a fixed-base manipulator advances its end-effector along a prescribed ray while satisfying position, orientation, joint-limit, and collision constraints.

Consider an $n$-DoF manipulator with joint configuration $\mathbf{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$. Let $\mathbf{p}(\mathbf{q})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}$ denote the end-effector position and $\mathbf{z}(\mathbf{q})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}$ the TCP local $z$-axis. A task is specified by $\mathbf{c}=[\mathbf{p}_{0},\mathbf{d},\mathbf{n}]\in\mathbb{R}^{9}$, where $\mathbf{p}_{0}$ is the starting position and $\mathbf{d}$ and $\mathbf{n}$ are unit vectors defining the motion direction and task-plane normal. The prescribed path is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{P}=\{\mathbf{p}_{0}+s\mathbf{d}:s\geq 0\},
\end{equation}
and the end-effector advances along it at commanded speed $v$ with control period $\Delta t$. The formulation below focuses on straight rays; whether the initial-configuration selector, conditioned on the sampled path ahead, transfers to curved path families is evaluated separately in Section~\ref{sec:ood}.

Given an initial configuration $\mathbf{q}_{0}$ and a redundancy-resolution policy $\pi$, execution terminates at the first violation of a feasibility constraint. Let $\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{q}_{0},\pi;\mathbf{c})$ denote the Cartesian length completed before termination. We define the \emph{continuous kinematic reachability} of task $\mathbf{c}$ as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ckr}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CKR}}(\mathbf{c})
=
\sup_{\mathbf{q}_{0},\,\pi}
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{q}_{0},\pi;\mathbf{c}),
\end{equation}
subject to
\begin{align}
&\operatorname{dist}\!\left(
\mathbf{p}(\mathbf{q}_{t}),\mathcal{P}
\right)
\leq \epsilon_{\mathrm{pos}},
\label{eq:pos_constraint}\\
&\arccos\!\left(
\mathbf{z}(\mathbf{q}_{t})^{\top}\mathbf{n}
\right)
\leq \theta_{\max},
\label{eq:ori_constraint}\\
&\mathbf{q}_{\min}
\preceq \mathbf{q}_{t}
\preceq \mathbf{q}_{\max},
\label{eq:jl_constraint}\\
&\mathbf{q}_{t}\in\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{free}},
\label{eq:collision_constraint}
\end{align}
where $\epsilon_{\mathrm{pos}}$ and $\theta_{\max}$ denote the position and orientation tolerances, and $\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{free}}$ denotes the collision-free configuration set. In practice, $\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{q}_{0},\pi;\mathbf{c})$ is the stroke realized by a particular initial configuration and controller, whereas $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CKR}}$ is the optimal continuously executable extent.

Because only the $3$-DoF position is tracked, the manipulator retains a null space when the position Jacobian $\mathbf{J}$ has full row rank and $n>3$. The commanded joint velocity is decomposed as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:nullspace}
\dot{\mathbf{q}}
=
\mathbf{J}_{\lambda}^{+}\dot{\mathbf{x}}
+
\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{q})\mathbf{u},
\qquad
\mathbf{J}\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{0},
\quad
\mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{I},
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{J}_{\lambda}^{+}$ is the damped pseudoinverse of the position Jacobian, $\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{q})$ is an orthonormal basis of its null space, and $\mathbf{u}$ contains the null-space coordinates. Thus, all controllers share the same task-space motion and differ only in how the null-space command is selected.

We bound the null-space command as
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{u}=a_{\max}\mathbf{a},
\qquad
\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{A}:=[-1,1]^{m},
\end{equation}
where $m=n-3$ for a full-row-rank position Jacobian. The resulting kinematics are control-affine:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:affine}
\dot{\mathbf{q}}
=
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{q};\mathbf{c})
+
\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{q})\mathbf{a},
\qquad
\mathbf{f}:=\mathbf{J}_{\lambda}^{+}\dot{\mathbf{x}},
\quad
\mathbf{G}:=a_{\max}\mathbf{B}.
\end{equation}

Equation~(\ref{eq:ckr}) therefore contains the two decisions addressed by the proposed framework: selecting the initial configuration $\mathbf{q}_{0}$ and selecting the bounded null-space command throughout the resulting continuous motion.


\section{Extreme Motion Generation}
\label{sec:hjb}

Extreme motion generation contains two coupled decisions. Before execution,
an initial task-feasible configuration must be selected; during execution,
the redundancy must be resolved at every increment. We address both through
a candidate-and-rank structure. The first stage generates feasible initial
configurations and learns to rank them from rollout outcomes. The second
stage generates candidate null-space motions from the kinematic model and
ranks their successor states using a scalar value estimate.

\subsection{Exit-Time Optimal Control Formulation}

The stroke terminates at the first constraint violation, so its horizon is
not prescribed in advance. Let $\Omega$ denote the feasible configuration
set defined by the position, orientation, joint-limit, and collision
constraints of Section~III, and define the exit time
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:exit-time}
\tau_{\mathrm{exit}}
=
\inf\{t\geq 0:\mathbf{q}(t)\notin\Omega\}.
\end{equation}
For a fixed task condition $\mathbf{c}$, let $r(\mathbf{q};\mathbf{c})$
denote the instantaneous progress rate along the prescribed path. We define
the discounted exit-time value
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:value}
V(\mathbf{q}_{0};\mathbf{c})
=
\sup_{\mathbf{a}(\cdot)}
\int_{0}^{\tau_{\mathrm{exit}}}
e^{-\beta t}
r(\mathbf{q}(t);\mathbf{c})\,\mathrm{d}t,
\end{equation}
where $\beta>0$ and the supremum is over measurable commands in
$\mathcal{A}$. Under the fixed path-advance rate used in this work,
$r$ is constant while the trajectory remains feasible. Hence maximizing
Eq.~(\ref{eq:value}) is equivalent to delaying the first constraint
violation and therefore to maximizing the executed stroke.

This formulation also connects the two stages of the framework. Initial
configuration selection compares the future stroke attainable from
candidate start states, whereas motion generation compares the future
stroke attainable from candidate successor states.

\subsection{Initial Joint Configuration Selection}
\label{sec:select}

At the starting path point, multiple joint configurations may satisfy the
same task constraints while leading to substantially different executable
strokes. We refer to connected portions of this task-feasible
configuration set as \emph{branches}. Fixed-pose self-motion manifolds are
a special case of this structure, and joint limits may separate feasible
solutions into disconnected components~\cite{burdick1989characterization,
luck1993self,shimizu2008analytical}.

The selection stage therefore seeks a favorable initial configuration
rather than a locally optimal posture. Candidate configurations are
obtained from the IKSel table~\cite{yuan2026iksel} by querying
$N_{\mathrm{dir}}$ cone directions, reranking the retrieved solutions,
refining them, and retaining $K$ diverse candidates through farthest-point
selection.

Because the quality of an initial configuration is determined by the
subsequent stroke, the selector is supervised directly by rollout
outcomes. Each candidate in the training set is executed with the deployed
motion-generation controller of Section~\ref{sec:traj}, and the resulting
stroke length provides its target score. A permutation-equivariant network
then ranks the candidate set using each candidate together with a
mean-pooled set context. The score is additionally conditioned on a
\emph{road table} that samples the position, tangent, and cone axis of the
upcoming path every $10$\,cm of arc length up to $1.2$\,m ahead. At deployment, selection requires only candidate generation,
one network evaluation, and refinement; no rollout is performed online.

\subsection{Value-Guided Motion Generation}
\label{sec:traj}

After the initial configuration is selected, the trajectory is generated
incrementally. At every control interval, the task-space controller
advances the end-effector along the prescribed path, while the remaining
motion is generated in the null space of the position Jacobian.

All compared controllers use the same orthonormal null-space basis
$\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{q})$. To obtain a task-aligned parameterization,
directions associated with directional manipulability, joint-limit
centering, and orientation margin are projected into the null space and
orthonormalized; the remaining orthogonal directions complete the basis.
The null-space velocity is
\begin{equation}
\dot{\mathbf{q}}_{\mathrm{null}}
=
\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{q})\left(a_{\max}\mathbf{a}\right),
\end{equation}
so the controllers differ only in how $\mathbf{a}$ is selected.

The exit-time formulation provides a structured rule for this selection.
At points where $V$ is differentiable, the Hamilton--Jacobi--Bellman
equation associated with Eq.~(\ref{eq:value}) is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:hjb}
\beta V
=
r
+
\nabla_{\mathbf{q}}V^{\top}\mathbf{f}
+
\sup_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{A}}
\left(
\mathbf{G}^{\top}\nabla_{\mathbf{q}}V
\right)^{\top}\mathbf{a},
\qquad
V=0 \ \text{outside } \Omega .
\end{equation}
Because $r$ and $\mathbf{f}$ are independent of $\mathbf{a}$, the command
appears linearly in the final term. Define
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sigma}
\boldsymbol{\sigma}(\mathbf{q})
=
\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{q})^{\top}
\nabla_{\mathbf{q}}V(\mathbf{q})
\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.
\end{equation}
For the box-bounded command set $\mathcal{A}=[-1,1]^{m}$, the pointwise
Hamiltonian maximization is attained by a vertex command.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:vertex}
Let $W:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}$ be differentiable and define
$\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{W}
=
\mathbf{G}^{\top}\nabla_{\mathbf{q}}W$.
Then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:prop}
\max_{\mathbf{a}\in[-1,1]^{m}}
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{W}^{\top}\mathbf{a}
=
\max_{\mathbf{a}\in\{-1,1\}^{m}}
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{W}^{\top}\mathbf{a}
=
\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{W}\|_{1}.
\end{equation}
An optimizer can be chosen as
$\mathbf{a}=\operatorname{sgn}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{W})$, with arbitrary
vertex values on components for which $\sigma_{W,i}=0$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{IEEEproof}
For $\mathbf{a}\in[-1,1]^{m}$,
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{W}^{\top}\mathbf{a}
=
\sum_{i}\sigma_{W,i}a_{i}
\leq
\sum_{i}|\sigma_{W,i}|.
\end{equation}
Equality is attained by choosing
$a_i=\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma_{W,i})$ whenever
$\sigma_{W,i}\neq 0$, which is a vertex-valued command.
\end{IEEEproof}

\begin{remark}[Scope]
\label{rem:bangbang}
Proposition~\ref{prop:vertex} concerns the pointwise Hamiltonian
maximization. It does not by itself establish equality between the
continuous box-constrained and vertex-constrained optimal-control
problems. When a switching component vanishes, an optimal vertex-valued
choice exists but is not unique. The result is derived in continuous time;
the finite-rate controller below uses its one-step counterpart.
\end{remark}

We estimate long-horizon state value using a standard actor--critic
training run. Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) trains a stochastic
vertex policy
$\pi_{\theta}(\mathbf{a}\mid\mathbf{o})$
together with a critic
$\widehat{V}_{\phi}(\mathbf{o})$.
The observation contains the normalized joint configuration and its
square, task vectors $\mathbf{d}$ and $\mathbf{n}$, tool-axis features,
and the previous command. The per-step reward is normalized path progress,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reward}
r_t
=
\operatorname{clip}\!\left(
\frac{
(\mathbf{p}_{t+1}-\mathbf{p}_{t})^{\top}\mathbf{d}
}{
v\Delta t
},
0,1
\right),
\end{equation}
with no terminal penalty. Its discounted return is the discrete-time
counterpart of the exit-time objective in Eq.~(\ref{eq:value}).

Direct deployment of $\pi_{\theta}$ serves as the reactive-policy
baseline. Our controller instead uses the critic to rank the successor
states generated by the vertex commands. Let
$F(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{a})$ denote the one-step kinematic transition under
a held command, and let
$\mathbf{o}^{+}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{c},\mathbf{a})$
denote the corresponding successor observation, with $\mathbf{a}$ placed
in its previous-command component. The deployed value controller is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:value-law}
\mathbf{a}_{V}(\mathbf{q})
=
\arg\max_{
\substack{
\mathbf{a}\in\{-1,1\}^{m}\\
F(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{a})\in\Omega
}
}
\widehat{V}_{\phi}
\left(
\mathbf{o}^{+}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{c},\mathbf{a})
\right).
\end{equation}
Thus, the continuous-time HJB analysis supplies the vertex candidate set,
while the finite-rate implementation compares the learned values of the
corresponding successor observations. Because the task-space controller commands the same nominal path
increment for all feasible vertices, we rank them by successor value in
the finite-rate implementation. With an approximate learned critic, Eq.~(\ref{eq:value-law})
is a model-based value-ranking rule rather than an exact policy-improvement
guarantee.

This construction avoids differentiating the learned critic directly.
Instead, the known kinematic model maps each candidate command to a
distinct successor state, and the critic is used only for long-horizon
ranking. The same candidate-ranking structure can also consume analytic
scalar fields, including the constraint-margin field used as a
training-free baseline in Section~V.

\section{Experiments and Analysis}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

Experiments are conducted on the 7-DoF xArm7, the 7-DoF Franka Research 3
(FR3), and the 6-DoF Cobotta using the \textit{One} robotic
system\footnote{https://github.com/wanweiwei07/one}, with a pen-shaped
tool attached to the end-effector. Each manipulator is evaluated on
$10{,}000$ held-out straight-line tasks specified by the starting position
$\mathbf{p}_0$, path direction $\mathbf{d}$, and task-plane normal
$\mathbf{n}$, sampled within that manipulator's own work range under a
minimum-feasibility screen; the three task sets are therefore not in
correspondence across manipulators. Training and evaluation tasks are
disjoint.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1em]
\renewcommand{\labelitemi}{$\triangleright$}

    \item \uline{Task and control parameters:}
    Unless otherwise specified, the orientation-cone half-angle is
    $\theta_{\max}=30^{\circ}$, the position tolerance is
    $\epsilon_{\mathrm{pos}}=20$\,mm, the commanded speed is
    $v=0.2$\,m/s, the control period is $\Delta t=50$\,ms, and the
    null-space authority is $a_{\max}=0.5$\,rad/s. All compared
    controllers share the same task-space tracking, joint-limit,
    orientation, and collision constraints.

    \item \uline{Initial configurations:}
    For the full-framework and selection experiments, the candidate pool
    contains $K=32$ configurations retrieved from
    $N_{\mathrm{dir}}=40$ directions using IKSel. The learned selector
    ranks these candidates, and the selected configuration is followed by
    one damped-least-squares refinement. Controller-only comparisons use
    the same fixed initial configuration for all methods to isolate the
    redundancy-resolution law.

    \item \uline{Classical controller:}
    The classical null-space controller uses weights $(0.8,0.4,0.2)$ for
    directional manipulability, joint-limit centering, and orientation
    regulation, respectively.

    \item \uline{Margin controller:}
    The analytic constraint-margin field uses temperature
    $\tau_{\mathrm{m}}=0.1$.

    \item \uline{Learned controllers:}
    Vertex-PPO is trained for $30$M environment steps separately for each
    manipulator, with discount $\gamma=0.99$ and $128$ parallel
    environments (about $1.75$\,h per manipulator on one RTX~4090). The
    proposed value controller uses the critic from the same training run
    to rank model-generated successor states, at a deployment latency of
    $15.5$\,ms per $50$\,ms decision ($14.8$\,ms for the margin law). All
    resolution laws share the same task-space controller and null-space
    parameterization.

    \item \uline{Evaluation metrics:}
    All performance is reported as the executed stroke and \emph{Ratio},
    the per-task ratio of the executed stroke to the task's numerical
    pointwise-reachable length $\ell^{\mathrm{pw}}$, reported in percent
    and summarized by its mean and 10th percentile (mean / p10). The classical controller
    appears as an ordinary row under the same metric. $\ell^{\mathrm{pw}}$
    is computed as follows. The task's path is sampled every $1$\,cm of
    arc length, and at each sample a numerical search asks whether any
    admissible configuration exists, i.e., one whose tool tip lies within
    $\epsilon_{\mathrm{pos}}$ of the sample, whose tool axis lies inside
    the orientation cone, and which respects the joint limits and
    self-collision clearance. The search projects warm starts drawn from a
    precomputed configuration table over $96$ cone-sampled tool directions
    with $16$ retries per sample, together with the previous sample's
    solution, and configurations traversed by the evaluated rollouts are
    additionally injected as feasibility witnesses;
    $\ell^{\mathrm{pw}}$ is the arc length of the first sample at which no
    admissible configuration is found. Because a failed search does not
    prove infeasibility, the reference is finally taken per task as the
    maximum of the search estimate and the executed stroke, so every
    reported Ratio is at most one by construction; where the adjustment
    binds, its median size is $4$\,mm and $96\%$ of all tasks lie within
    one march step of the search estimate, with the residual tail
    concentrated on high-curvature arcs where the pointwise search is
    conservative.
\end{itemize}

All evaluation tables compare the four path families illustrated in
Fig.~\ref{fig:families}: straight, arc, serpentine, and rotating-axis.
The redundancy-resolution controllers and critics are trained on
straight-line tasks only, and the initial-configuration selector is
trained on straight and arc tasks; every evaluated task instance is held
out from all training. Consequently, straight and arc results measure
generalization to \emph{unseen but in-distribution} tasks, whereas
serpentine and rotating-axis results measure generalization to
\emph{out-of-distribution} (OOD) families that appear in no training set;
for the motion-generation stage, the arc family is additionally zero-shot,
since the controllers never observe curved paths during training.
Straight-line results use $10{,}000$ tasks per manipulator, and each
curved family uses $2{,}500$ tasks.

\subsection{Redundancy-Resolution Comparison}
\label{sec:ladder}

We first isolate the motion-generation stage by comparing the proposed
critic-guided value law with five redundancy-resolution baselines. For
each task, all methods start from the same initial configuration and share
the same task-space controller, feasibility constraints, null-space
parameterization, and control period. Thus, differences in executed stroke
are attributable to the redundancy-resolution law.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1em]
\renewcommand{\labelitemi}{$\triangleright$}

    \item \uline{Zero Null-Space (Zero):}
    No secondary motion is applied, i.e.,
    $\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{0}$. This baseline measures the stroke obtained from
    task-space tracking alone.

    \item \uline{Classical Gradient Resolution (Classical):}
    A conventional null-space gradient controller combines directional
    manipulability, joint-limit centering, and orientation regulation using
    the weights specified in Section~\ref{sec:setup}. Its command performs
    gradient ascent on the weighted secondary field,
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:secondary}
    \boldsymbol{\xi}
    = k_{\mu}\nabla_{\mathbf{q}} w_{\mathbf{d}}(\mathbf{q})
    - k_{\mathrm{jl}}
    \frac{\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{q}_{\mathrm{mid}}}
         {\mathbf{q}_{\max}-\mathbf{q}_{\min}}
    + k_{\theta}\, g(\theta)\,
    \nabla_{\mathbf{q}}\!\bigl(\mathbf{z}(\mathbf{q})^{\top}\mathbf{n}\bigr),
    \end{equation}
    applied as $\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{cl}} \propto
    \mathbf{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\xi}$, where $w_{\mathbf{d}}$ is the
    directional manipulability along $\mathbf{d}$, the second term centers
    each joint in its range, and the gate $g(\theta)$ activates the
    orientation term as the tool axis approaches the cone boundary. It
    represents instantaneous geometric redundancy resolution without
    learned long-horizon information.

    \item \uline{Analytic Margin Resolution (Margin):}
    The same one-step vertex-selection structure as the proposed controller
    is used, but successor configurations are ranked by the training-free
    constraint-margin field
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:margin-field}
    \Phi_{\mathrm{m}}(\mathbf{q})
    =
    -\tau_{\mathrm{m}}
    \log
    \sum_{j}
    \exp\left(
    -m_{j}(\mathbf{q})/\tau_{\mathrm{m}}
    \right),
    \end{equation}
    where $m_j$ denotes a normalized joint-limit or orientation-cone margin.
    This baseline retains the model-based candidate ranking structure but
    uses only instantaneous constraint information.

    \item \uline{Continuous-Action PPO (Continuous PPO):}
    A PPO policy over the continuous command box $[-1,1]^{m}$ with a
    tanh-squashed Gaussian head and the tanh-corrected entropy bonus,
    trained under the same observations, reward, and budget as the vertex
    policy and deployed through its deterministic mean. It measures what
    direct reactive learning achieves without the vertex restriction.

    \item \uline{Reactive Vertex Policy (Vertex PPO):}
    The vertex-valued PPO policy of Section~\ref{sec:traj} directly maps the
    current observation to a null-space command.

    \item \uline{Hybrid Reactive Policy (Vertex PPO+Classical):}
    The same vertex-PPO policy is used away from the joint-limit boundary, while
    the classical controller takes over within a thin boundary region
    defined by normalized joint-limit distance, with hysteresis thresholds
    $0.98/0.94$.

    \item \uline{Critic-Guided Value Resolution (Value, Ours):}
    The proposed controller of Eq.~\ref{eq:value-law} uses the critic from
    the same vertex-PPO training run to rank feasible successor observations
    generated by the one-step kinematic model and selects the
    highest-valued vertex command.
\end{itemize}

Table~\ref{tab:mainresult} reports the results per path family for each
manipulator; on the curved families every learned method runs zero-shot.
Classical gradient resolution provides only a modest
improvement over zero null-space motion, whereas the analytic margin law
substantially extends the stroke on all three manipulators. Direct
deployment of the vertex-PPO policy remains below the margin law, while the
critic-guided value controller achieves the longest mean stroke on every
manipulator and every path family, although all learned methods are
trained on straight tasks only.

The Vertex PPO and Value rows are particularly informative because they use the
policy and critic obtained from the same actor--critic training run. Their
comparison therefore isolates two ways of consuming the learned
information: direct reactive action prediction through the policy, and
successor-state ranking through the critic combined with the kinematic
model. The consistently stronger performance of the latter supports the
division adopted by our motion-generation stage: the critic provides
long-horizon state evaluation, while the model performs local candidate
generation and discrimination.

\subsection{Generalization and Applications}

\subsubsection{Path-Type Generalization}
\label{sec:ood}

We summarize how the learned initial-configuration selector transfers
across path families. The selector is trained on $20{,}000$ tasks,
comprising $10{,}000$ straight and $10{,}000$ arc tasks in equal
proportion, with all $K$ candidates of every training task rolled out to
produce the ranking labels; it is then evaluated on $2{,}500$ held-out
tasks from each of the four families. The Learned Selector rows of
Table~\ref{tab:ablate_seed} distinguish unseen in-distribution tasks
(straight, arc) from OOD tasks belonging to the serpentine and
rotating-axis families that are absent from training. The results show that the achieved Ratio is governed by path curvature
rather than by training membership: the OOD rotating-axis family, whose
centerline is straight, matches the in-distribution straight family
($93.0\%$ vs $94.0\%$ in mean Ratio), and the OOD serpentine family even
exceeds the in-distribution arc family ($91.9\%$ vs $88.8\%$). On every family the learned
selector remains the strongest non-oracle ranking rule.


\subsubsection{Cross-Arm Transfer of the Learned Value}
\label{sec:crossarm}

We next evaluate how manipulator-specific the learned value is by
transferring the \emph{critic} between the two 7-DoF manipulators without
retraining. The test manipulator keeps its own kinematic model, task-space
controller, constraints, and null-space basis; only the scalar field that
ranks the successor states is replaced by the critic trained on the other
arm. The Cobotta is excluded because its null-space dimension is $m=3$.

\subsubsection{Implication for Manipulator Base Placement}
\label{sec:place}

Continuous kinematic reachability also induces a task-aware criterion for
manipulator base placement: for a candidate base pose, the required path
is transformed into the robot frame and evaluated with the same
continuous-path formulation, and the pose is feasible for the task if the
complete path can be executed as a single continuous stroke. Unlike
waypoint-wise reachability, this criterion accounts for the continuity of
the joint trajectory and the choice of initial configuration, and
therefore distinguishes poses from which the path is continuously
executable from those whose points are merely individually reachable.


\subsection{Real-World Validation}
\label{sec:real}

Because the proposed framework is kinematic, the physical experiments are designed to assess whether the generated extreme motions remain executable after accounting for hardware motion limits. Trajectories are generated offline by the learned selector and critic-guided value controller, then time-parameterized using Ruckig~\cite{berscheid2021jerk} under the manufacturer's joint velocity and acceleration limits. The resulting trajectories are executed on a Franka Research 3 through its joint-position interface.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Conclusions and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

In this paper, we studied \textit{continuous kinematic reachability}, the maximum extent of a prescribed path that can be realized by a single continuous joint trajectory. Extreme motion generation was formulated as two coupled decisions: selecting the initial configuration and resolving redundancy throughout execution. We evaluated the realized stroke across three manipulators and used numerical pointwise reachability as a task-specific reference.

For redundancy resolution, the exit-time formulation and HJB analysis show that pointwise Hamiltonian maximization admits vertex-valued null-space commands. This structure enables a one-step kinematic model to generate candidate successor states that are ranked by a learned critic. Across all three manipulators, critic-guided resolution achieves longer strokes than the corresponding reactive policy and the training-free baselines. For initial configuration selection, rollout-supervised ranking further improves the achievable stroke and transfers to path families absent from selector training.

Together, these results support a candidate-and-rank framework in which model-based candidate generation is combined with learned long-horizon evaluation. The same continuous-path formulation also induces a task-aware criterion for manipulator base placement. Future work will extend the framework to dynamic constraints and paths specified online.
